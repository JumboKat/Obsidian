### Prelude
Since the mid-17th century, Dutch settlers had established the **Cape Colony** settlement in what is now South Africa. They called themselves Afrikaners and spoke their own dialect of **Afrikaans**, but are more often referred to as **Boers**, meaning "peasant" in Dutch. They seized lands from and enslaved the natives of South Africa. After the Battle of Cape Town in 1806 during the Napoleonic Wars, Great Britain maintained permanent control over the colony after 1815, but conflicts over language, culture, and legislation arose between the Boers and the British, which came to a head in 1834 when Britain outlawed slavery. 

![[Pasted image 20260531002432.png]]
This prompted a mass exodus of Boers known as the , who relied on slaves to run their farms, out of the colony to found new independent settlements in the east, an event known as the **Great Trek (1835-1840)**. There they founded two new republics: the **Orange Free State** and the **South African Republic (ZAR)**, where they continued the practice of slavery and stole land from local Africans.

![[Pasted image 20260531002554.png]]
As Britain expanded its territory, it came to share borders with the Boer republics. In 1887, the British announced their formal annexation of the ZAR and renamed it as the **Transvaal Colony**; the colony was economically weak after years of fighting its neighbors, and the British feared they would succumb to the Zulu or the Germans to the north. After three years of failed diplomacy by the local Boers to remove the British, the Transvaal Boers resorted to violence, resulting in the **First Boer War (1880-1881)**. The British were not prepared for the Boers' guerilla tactics, and believing that the colony wasn't worth the cost of fighting a drawn-out war, the British agreed to withdraw and grant them self-rule (aside from foreign policy), on the condition they would remain a part of the British Empire.

Then came the **Transvaal Gold Rush** from 1884 to 1886 after prospectors discovered huge deposits of gold, attracting immigrants from all over the British Empire. Soon British statesmen and businessmen started to pay attention to the region, including Cape Colony Prime Minister **Cecil Rhodes**, who led the campaign to bring the republics under British rule and secure their natural resources. However, he only managed to reveal to the Boers the threat of annexation by Great Britain when, in 1895, he conducted mercenaries to carry out the failed **Jameson Raid** on Boer territory. Regardless, he continued to pressure the Boer republics into granting legal and territorial concessions.
### Outbreak of War
The Boers, in fear of British aggression, began purchasing weapons from the Germans, an act that stoked British fears of German expansion. British troops began amassing in the nearby colony of **Natal**, and by 1899, it was clear to the Boers that invasion was imminent, and so decided to make the first move. On October 11th, 1899, the Boers made a preemptive strike against the British, marking the beginning of the **Second Boer War**.

The Boers did not have a professional military and relied on local militias. When the war began, the government conscripted all able-bodied men between the ages 16-60. While most were expected to bring their own rifle and horse, the government had also distributed the thousands of Mauser rifles that they had purchased from the Germans. While the Boers lacked any formal military training, they were formidable marksmen, having developed their skills hunting game and competing in shooting tournaments during peacetime. Every Boer was skilled as a mounted rifleman, able to move around quickly to take key positions, before dismounting and firing their rifles with great accuracy. Several decades later, Winston Churchill would name the British special forces "commandos," after the Afrikaans word for military unit. The Boers also possessed modern German and French artillery guns.

While they were well-armed, skilled, motivated to fight for independence and 60,000 strong, they were highly dependent on imports from Europe to support their large population, and could not afford a long war of attrition, and feared any opportunities of an African uprising or invasion by the Zulus. Hence, their goal was to score a quick, decisive victory against the British.

The British not only could field much greater numbers than the Boers (with 250,000 troops available), but were well experienced in colonial warfare, having won many conflicts in the past by taking advantage of their superior technology, military discipline, and tactics based on tight formations and concentrated barrages. However, they lacked experience in the face of the modern rifles and artillery fielded by the Boers, something they overlooked to their detriment. Despite their defeat to the Transvaal Boers in 1881, British military command believed the war would be won swiftly and decisively, expecting the Boers to be weak and undisciplined. Native Africans were employed by both sides during the war; the Boers used African slave labour to transport supplies and dig trenches, while the British hired thousands of Africans to help carry out logistics, tracking, and scouting.
### The Boer Offensive
![[Pasted image 20260601180713.png]]
In their initial strike, the Boers attacked the British on four fronts: the railway town of **Mafeking**, the diamond-mining town of **Kimberley**, the Midland Region of Cape Colony to disrupt rail transport, and into the Natal colony, where the main Boer offensive took place. This is where the bulk of the British forces were (about 15,000 troops, at this time the Boers outnumbered the British, with 40,000 Boers vs 20,000 British), as they planned to use it as a staging ground for their invasion of the Boer colonies.
#### Battle of Talana Hill
![[Pasted image 20260601181423.png]]
At Natal, the British were poorly deployed, and on October 20th, the Boers took to **Talana Hill** and bombarded a detachment of 4000 men. While the British were able to eventually drive the Boers off with a cavalry charge, they suffered heavy casualties, and were then in danger of being cut off from the main British garrison at **Ladysmith**. Though they faced attacks from the mobile Boer units during their retreat, they were able to defeat the Boers through skilled coordination and the use of cavalry.
#### Battle of Ladysmith
By the end of October, the Boer advance had not slowed, and British commander **George White**, was faced with making the decision between retreating to preserve his army, or attack the Boers head-on and attempt to turn the tide of the war in a decisive victory. He chose the latter, attacking the Boers in a full frontal assault on October 30th at the Battle of Ladysmith. The British were uncoordinated, however, and White was forced to retreat to Ladysmith, where they were then besieged by the Boers.
### British Relief Attempts
![[Pasted image 20260601182915.png]]
In November, a British reinforcement army of 45,000 regulars under the command of General **Redvers Buller**. On his arrival, he split his force into three contingents: 15,000 to relieve Kimberley, 5,000 to secure the Midland region, and 25,000 under his command to break the siege at Ladysmith. As the British had to make the journey via railway, the Boers were aware of their arrival and had ample time to prepare defensive positions to meet each of the British contingents. What followed was a string of British defeats on all fronts during what was called the **Black Week**.

On December 10th, the British sent to the Cape Midlands lost their way during a night march and ended up out in the open in the middle of the Boer position. At the ensuing **Battle of Stormberg**, they were cut down by heavy rifle-fire and forced to surrender. On December 11th, the contingent sent to Kimberley engaged the Boers at the **Battle of Magersfontein**. They too attempted a nighttime maneuver, but were slowed by a storm, and by sunrise, they were exposed in the open and pinned down by the Boers, until eventually they were forced into an ill-organized retreat.

![[Pasted image 20260601195816.png]]
On December 15th, at the **Battle of Colenso**, the main force under Buller attacked the Boers positioned along the **Tugela River**. British artillery moved up too far and were knocked out, and when British infantry attempted an assault across the river, they were ambushed and fired upon from three sides. By mid morning, the battle had ended with heavy British losses, while the Boers suffered minimal casualties. While Boer confidence was at its peak, the tragic losses and humiliation strengthened British resolve.
### British Renewed Offensive
In January 1900, the British war hero Lord Frederick Roberts arrived along with tens of thousands of reinforcements from all over the empire, including Canada, New Zealand, Australia. One of these soldiers was **Mohandas Gandhi**, who served with the Indian Ambulance Corps. Roberts began reforming the British strategy to counter the Boers weaponry and tactics, including a transition away from railway transport. The British made their new offensive on February 10th, 1900. At Kimberley, an army of 50,000 British soldiers were able to break through the Boer siege lines and threatened to cut off their escape, forcing the Boers to end the siege. The Boers, with supply wagons in tow, opted to dig in at **Paardeberg**, where they were surrounded by the British. After a 10-day siege, they surrendered, with four thousand Boers taken prisoner, a fatal blow to the Boers' strength. Meanwhile, Buller made renewed attempts to relieve the troops at Ladysmith, but they were repelled at **Spion Kop** in January and again at **Vaal Krantz** in February. In late February, however, Buller managed to break through at the Tugela Heights. The British made coordinated use of artillery and infantry in step-by-step attacks, resembling tactics used in the First World War, and were able to lift the siege at Ladysmith on February 27th.

![[Pasted image 20260602142952.png]]
With the Boers now weakened, the British made a great push into the Boer territories, taking **Bloemfontein** in March and **Pretoria** in June. The Boers made their last defense at **Bergendal** in late August, but could at no point during the invasion match the British in numbers or firepower. By September, the British had successfully occupied the Boer republics, and scattered their forces. Roberts declared the war won and returned to England, where he was hailed as a hero. However, the Boers had not lost determination, and Boer generals looked towards other forms of resistance.
### Guerilla War
Some Boer generals like **Christiaan de Wet** of the Orange Free State were in favour of a guerilla war; the Boers were greatly disadvantaged when facing the numerical superiority of the British in traditional battles. There had already been a precedent for the success of guerilla warfare, like the attack on an armoured train carrying war correspondent **Winston Churchill**, and on March 31st, 1900, de Wet had proven the effectiveness of guerilla tactics at the **Battle of Sanna's Post**, where his kommandos were able to successfully ambush a much larger British force equipped with artillery with minimal casualties. Throughout the year 1900, de Wet would employ hit-and-run tactics against British columns and sabotage railway lines, taking full advantage of the Boers' superior mobility. After their defeat at Bergendal in August, the rest of the Boer generals took note of de Wet's strategy, and in October, the guerilla campaign was in full force under the leadership of **Louis Botha**.

![[Pasted image 20260602181847.png]]
The Boers focused their attacks on the British railway, sabotaging rail lines and hijacking unguarded trains. The British had no counter for the guerilla attacks. Instead, they responded with scorched earth. Starting mid 1900, Lord Roberts ordered the destruction of all farms suspected to be supplying the Boers. Then from December of 1900 onward, his successor **Herbert Kitchener** ramped up the response, having all Boer-owned farms torched regardless of their allegiances. Thousands of farms were destroyed, and 150,000 civilians lost their homes. In addition, Kitchener implemented the **blockhouse system**, which was a string of manned pillboxes linked together by barbed wire. 8,000 of these were built, mainly along rail lines and river crossings, establishing a permanent, defensive perimeter that restricted the Boers' mobility.

As the guerilla war dragged on, many atrocities were committed by both sides. Boer kommandos raided African villages and pro-British farms, while the British continued to burn down Boer farms. Combat became limited to ambushes on British columns, with the fighting becoming brutal and carried out at close-quarters. The Boers looted weapons and equipment from slain British soldiers, and the British executed any Boers caught with British gear.
### Concentration Camps
![[Pasted image 20260602184516.png]]
As Kitchener's scorched earth policy destroyed Boer homes, the British sought to control the growing wave of refugees by placing them in concentration camps. Starting in May 1900, displaced Boers were placed in temporary camps, and in December 1900 the system was made official. While initially intended to be centres for food, medical aid, and shelter, the conditions in the camps soon became abhorrent. For one, they were severely understaffed; the military refused to provide officers, seeing it as a civilian issue, while civilian authorities ranked them as low-priority, and could not find sufficient administrative and medical personnel. Thus, the camps were poorly managed and lacked resources, and were often built in areas that lacked adequate sanitation. By far the most pressing issue was overcrowding; the camps interned 44,000 refugees in March 1901, but by December this figure had risen to 110,000. What followed were major outbreaks of lice and disease, including diphtheria and malaria.

In May 1901, British aid worker **Emily Hobhouse** exposed the horrific conditions to the global public, garnering worldwide outrage against Great Britain. **Millicent Fawcet** was appointed to investigate the concentration camp system and confirmed Hobhouse's claims and recommended major reforms. From November 1901, conditions began steadily improving under formal civilian management. By the time the war had ended in May 1902, the mortality rate in the camps was actually lower than that of the pre-war Boer republics. 

![[Pasted image 20260602184501.png]]
A parallel camp system was made for Black civilians. These were proposed to be self-sufficient and received even less funding and attention than the Boer camps. These refugees would not see the same improvements as their Boer counterparts; the Fawcet commission had ignored them entirely. 

By early 1902, the remaining Boer fighters were at their limit. The British scorched earth campaign had denied them of much of their supplies, and the blockhouse system had stripped away their mobility advantage. On top of that, they had also been struggling to support 10,000 civilians who were sheltering in the wilderness to avoid British capture. Most of them lacked basic human necessities. In some cases, conditions were so bad that the Boers gave them up to the concentration camps. At this point, there was no possible road to victory for the Boers, and any further fighting would only prolong the suffering. 
### Treaty of Vereeniging
With no other choice, the Boers negotiated peace with the British, resulting in the **Treaty of Vereeniging** in May 1902. The terms were as follows:
- The Boers were to surrender but be granted amnesty.
- The British promised to protect the Dutch language.
- The Boer republics would become a part of the British Empire, but be granted self-rule.

Discussion on black voting rights were delayed until after self-governance was established.
### Aftermath
The Second Boer War had devastated the Boer republics. The British suffered 100,000 casualties, while the Boers suffered 30,000. In the concentration camps, at least 27,000 Boer civilians had died, of which 22,000 were children under the age of 16. At least 25,000 Black civilians had also died. Overall, 110,000 Boer civilians had been interned.

Having been unexpectedly long, costly, and brutal, the Second Boer War prompted the British to make military reforms and strengthen its position by forming new alliances with Japan, France, and Russia. Many of the officers and soldiers involved would also go on to fight in the First World War.
