### History of the Elevator
In the 1800s, architects began to build taller buildings with more floors, but were limited by the fact that higher levels could only be accessed by walking up stairs. This problem was solved with the introduction of **elevators**, or **lifts**.

![[Pasted image 20260515020042.png|428]]
The idea of using a machine to lift objects has been implemented throughout history, with the earliest tracing back to over 2000 years ago. **Hoists**, or **cranes**, have been used to lift heavy loads of materials and equipment, but a lack of safeguards made it unfit for lifting people.

![[Pasted image 20260515080628.png]]
This was all changed in 1853 when American industrialist **Elisha Otis** invented a safety mechanism.

![[Pasted image 20260515080742.png]]
The safety mechanism is comprised of a leaf spring, pivot arms, and teeth running along the walls of both sides of the lift.

![[Pasted image 20260515080838.png|349]]![[Pasted image 20260515081140.png|329]]
As the lift is pulled upward, the pivot arms are pulled upward and out of contact of the teeth, allowing the lift to rise. If the rope suddenly loses tension, the leaf spring is pulled down, pushing the arms downward and in contact with the metal teeth, stopping the lift from falling it into the ground. 

In 1854, Otis demonstrated his invention to the public at the World Fair. He lifted himself up on the elevator, and had the rope cut, after which he only fell a few centimetres; the demonstration had successfully convinced the public that elevators were safe for human riders. Since then, elevator shafts have become the core of most buildings, enabling much taller architectures seen in the likes of skyscrapers.
### The Elevator System
The elevator system is located in a vertical space called the **shaft** or the **hoistway**. At the bottom is a hole called the **elevator pit**. 
![[Pasted image 20260515083228.png]]
The **cab** is the box that carries passengers and moves up and down the shaft along the **guide rails** via contacting wheels called **guide rollers**.

![[Pasted image 20260515083432.png]]
There are two main types of elevators: **traction elevators**, which lift the cab using cables from above, and **hydraulic elevators** which push the cab up from underneath. 
### Hydraulic Elevators
 ![[Pasted image 20260515110634.png|288]]![[Pasted image 20260515110758.png|134]]
Beneath the elevator in a hydraulic system sits a cylinder and a piston. These extend downward to a length long enough to push the elevator up to the highest floor. 

![[Pasted image 20260515111011.png]]
Nearby in the machine room is a **pump unit** filled with oil. To ascend the elevator, oil is pumped through the pipes into the cylinder. The increase in pressure pushes the piston upward, raising the elevator. 

Hydraulic elevators are usually only found in shorter buildings, as its maximum height is limited by the length of the piston and the pressure needed to push the cab up.
### Traction Elevators
Traction elevators are used in very tall buildings; they can service buildings with over 100 floors in some cases.
#### Design
![[Pasted image 20260515111459.png|387]]![[Pasted image 20260515111555.png|288]]
The machine room is located at the very top and houses an electric motor. The motor pulls the cables by turning a special pulley called a **sheave**. One end of the cable connects to the cab, whilst the other to the **counterweight**. The counterweight massively reduces the strain on the motor. The motor simply tips the balance to move the cab up or down, with the counterweight moving opposite the cab. Without it, there would be a massive amount of tension on one side of the motor, requiring much more energy and strength to lift the cab. When the cab is at half capacity, both it and the counterweight weigh an equal amount, and achieving an ideal balance. However, the weight can change frequently. 

Within the machine room also lies the control box, which serves as the brain of the elevator and controls the cab's up-and-down movement. Some traction elevators do not have a dedicated machine room; instead, the components are fitted at the top of the elevator shaft. These are called **machine room less (MRL) elevators**.
#### Safety Features
![[Pasted image 20260515112912.png|299]]
The **machine brake** is a braking mechanism that stops the motor and halts the cab's movement. The cab is suspended by 4-8 cables, each of which is able to support the weight of a fully-loaded cab alone.

![[Pasted image 20260515231957.png|247]]![[Pasted image 20260515232141.png|187]]![[Pasted image 20260515232206.png|181]]
In the event that all cables did fail, the elevator relies on a modern version of Elisha Otis' safety device. At the bottom of the cab are two emergency brakes fitted with two clamps on either side of each guide rail, remaining just out of contact under normal conditions. They are attached to a lever which, when pulled upward, causes them to grip the guide rail and halt the elevator's descent. 

![[Pasted image 20260515232501.png|332]]![[Pasted image 20260515232552.png|321]]
The lever is connected and pulled by a separate **governor rope**, which connects to a device called the **overspeed governor** at the top. Like the main cable, this rope loops around a **governor sheave**. In the centre are two **flyweights** restrained by tension springs and surrounded by a ring of gear teeth. This device normally spins in tandem with the cab as it moves up and down. If the cab starts to fall too quickly, the governor sheave spins quickly as well. It spins faster and faster until the centrifugal force created overcomes the tension springs and pushes the flyweights outward, which then catch onto the gear teeth. This stops the spinning, which stops the cable. As the cab is still falling, the cable is yanked upward, activating the clamps and halting the cab's descent.

If you find yourself stuck in an elevator, do not try to pry the doors open. Simply press the emergency button and wait for assistance.
### Elevator Doors
There are two sets of doors in an elevator system. On each floor is the **hoistway door**. The **cab door** is found on the elevator cab itself. Both sets of doors will not open until the cab stops at the correct floor, and the elevator will not start moving until both doors are completely closed. They are both connected to sensors to prevent closing if something is in the way. 

![[Pasted image 20260515233739.png|193]]![[Pasted image 20260515233835.png|230]]![[Pasted image 20260515234117.png|261]]
The cab door is controlled by the **door operator** which sits on top of the cab. To open the door, gears spin to pull on a lever to slide the door open. The hoistway doors do not have their own opening mechanism, and can only be opened by the cab's door operator, which opens both in tandem.

![[Pasted image 20260515234259.png|218]]![[Pasted image 20260515234328.png|219]]![[Pasted image 20260515234453.png|109]]![[Pasted image 20260515234513.png|121]]
Each hoistway door has an **interlock**, ensuring it doesn't open unless the cab is behind it. The interlock has a beak which, when down, prevents the door from opening. The beak is connected to a bar that, when pushed up, lifts the beak and allows the door to open.
![[Pasted image 20260515234831.png|164]]![[Pasted image 20260515234847.png|149]]![[Pasted image 20260515234752.png|151]]![[Pasted image 20260515234810.png|140]]
Below next to the bar are two black **pickup rollers**. When the one on the left is pushed to the right, it rotates and pushes the bar up to unlock the door.

![[Pasted image 20260515235041.png|164]]![[Pasted image 20260515235136.png|199]]![[Pasted image 20260515235223.png|299]]
A piece called the **clutch** sides on the front of the cab door. As the cab moves up and down, the clutch glides around the rollers. When the cab stops on a floor and opens its door, the clutch catches on the pickup roller as the cab door slides open, unlocking the interlock and allowing both doors to slide together. For emergency or maintenance purposes, a key held by authorized personnel can be used to unlock the hoistway door.
