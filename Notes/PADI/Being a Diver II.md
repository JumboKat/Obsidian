### Seeing and Hearing
#### Apparent Size and Distance
![[Pasted image 20260522101508.png]]
Water is about 800 times denser than air, causing light to change direction (refract) when it enters your mask from water. This causes an apparent 1/3rd magnification, making objects appear larger and/or closer.
#### Colour Loss and Scattering
Water also affects light by reflecting, scattering, and absorbing it; as you go deeper, it gets darker. 
![[Pasted image 20260522102017.png]]
Water absorbs light directly, but not uniformly; it absorbs some colours more than others. The farther light travels through water, the more colour it loses. Red is lost first, then orange, yellow, green, and finally blue. This also occurs when looking at distant objects, which tend to seem more colourful as you get closer.
#### Hearing Underwater
Water transmits sound more efficiently than air, allowing it to travel faster (over four times faster) and over much longer distances. This makes sounds that you wouldn't normally hear at the surface sound relatively close underwater. Thus, it is difficult to determine the source of a sound underwater. You may be able to tell the relative change in distance by a sound growing louder or fainter, even if you don't know the direction.
### Swimming and Moving
Water is much denser than air, and takes more effort to move in. It takes four times as much energy to double your speed. Thus, the faster you try to move, the faster you will tire. To move efficiently, move slowly and steadily, avoiding rapid and/or jerky moves. **Streamlining**, which involves wearing your gear close with nothing dangling, and swimming relatively level in water, reduces drag and in turn the energy you expend underwater.

Having correct trim allows you to stay neutrally buoyant and level in the water, which reduces drag and allows you to glide smoothly though the water. At the surface, inflating your BCD will allow you to float effortlessly. 
### Staying Warm
Water absorbs ore heat than air of the same temperature, absorbing it about 20 times faster. This means you can chill rapidly even in water as warm as 30ºC. Without an exposure suit, you will get cold after a while. One is generally needed when diving in water 24ºC or cooler. There are different types of exposure protection:
- **Dry suits** provide the most insulation and are used for the coolest water/longest dives.
- **Wet suits** allow for dives in a wide temperature range, as they come in different thicknesses. Select styles can cover more or less of your body.

**Hypothermia** is a serious condition in which the body cools so much it can no longer function properly. Continuous and uncontrolled shivering is a symptom of hypothermia, and if experienced, you should immediately exit the water, dry off, and seek warmth.
### Breathing Effectively Underwater
Because the air you breathe underwater is denser than at the surface, it requires more effort to breathe. The denser the air and the faster you breathe, the more energy you use. Therefore, it is most effective to breathe *slowly and deeply*.
#### Dead Air
![[Pasted image 20260523104736.png]]
When you breathe, air passes through many parts of the airway, but only the lungs facilitate gas exchange. The air passages that do not take part in gas exchange are called **dead air space**. The first portion of air you inhale is actually leftover air from your last exhalation, and is higher in carbon dioxide. Dive equipment adds additional dead air space. Taking rapid and shallow breaths causes you to rebreathe a greater proportion of dead air with each breath, requiring you to use more energy to meet the body's gas exchange requirements; breathing slowly and deeply reduces the proportion of dead air in each breath.

When you are relaxed, you breathe slowly and deeply. Ensuring you breathe in such a way helps you stay relaxed, and can help you calm down if you feel anxious. It also helps you stay clear-headed and improves decision-making.
#### Airway Control
It is common to have residual water in the regulator or snorkel. Airway control allows you to breathe air past the water without drawing it into your airway. Assume that after you clear your regulator or snorkel, there will be some water leftover. There are two techniques for airway control:
1. Inhale slowly. Water will tend to stay in the mouthpiece (as it is heavier than air) while the air moves past. It helps to look downward slightly.
2. Touch your tongue to the roof of your mouth as you inhale. The tongue will block water as air flows around it. It also helps to look down slightly.
You can use either or both of these methods. If you get a few drops of water in the back of your throat, you will get an urge to cough; hold your regulator or snorkel in place when you cough.
#### Overexertion
**Overexertion** can be alarming and lead to panic and accidents. You can avoid/prevent it by recognizing its symptoms, which include:
- fatigue
- laboured breathing
- a feeling of suffocation or air starvation
- weakness
- anxiety
- headache
- muscle cramping
- a tendency to panic
Overexertion usually occurs after prolonged, elevated effort (for example, fighting a current), which prompts the diver to breathe dense air faster than their equipment can provide it. Breathing slowly, deeply, and continuously will prevent overexertion, as well as avoiding lengthy, physical stress on the body. If breathing rate begins to rise, stop and rest before it becomes laboured.

In the event you overexert yourself, stop and signal your buddy to rest. Avoid any further activity, which will worsen it. If you're at the surface, inflate your BCD and drop your weights so you can float without effort. Signal the boat to pick you up, if necessary. If underwater, hold onto something or rest on the bottom where you won't damage sensitive aquatic life.
### The Buddy System
Dives should be planned in advance; discuss what you want to accomplish, hazards to avoid, and what to do if a problem arises.
#### Predive Safety Check
You and your buddies gear up at the same time and can assist each other. After everyone has kitted up, you go through the **predive safety check**. You can remember it as BWRAF, or "Begin With Review And Friend"
#### Buddy Separation
During a dive, you and your buddy(ies) should ideally stay within a couple metres. A good rule of thumb is to be able to reach each other in two seconds. Buddy separation occurs when buddies can't see each other, or they can see each other but are too far away to provide quick assistance. In lower visibility, it is easy to lose sight of each other. If you get separated, look for each other for no more than a minute. If you are unable to relocate each other, ascend and reunite on the surface. Even when diving with three or more people, if only one gets separated from the others, all divers should follow the procedures for reuiniting.
### Swimming at the Surface
You will commonly spend time at the surface with your BCD partially inflated at the start of the dive before you descend, and at the end after you ascend. You may do this while waiting for your buddies to enter, or for your turn to exit. You may also swim at the surface if you ascend and descend some distance from where you enter and exit the water.

It is a good habit to keep your mask on and a mouthpiece in place when you're in water too deep to stand in; under stress, you tend to fall back on your habits. You may need to see and breathe with your face in the water in stressful conditions. Keeping your mask and mouthpiece on can also protect your eyes and airway from unexpected splashes or waves, and you are less likely to lose them. If you will be at the surface for more than a few minutes, switch to your snorkel to conserve your air supply.
