All batteries have a negative terminal (**anode**) and a positive terminal (**cathode**). Negatively-charged electrons flow from the anode to the cathode. In a **lithium-ion battery**, electrons come from the element lithium. 
### The Anode
At the anode, lithium is stored between layers of carbon graphite. Graphite has a crystal structure of layered planes that allows lithium to be intercalated, or wedged in between the layers. The graphite provides a stable space for storing the lithium. As lithium's atomic number is 3, it has one unstable valence electron, meaning it has a very high tendency to lose electrons. When there is an available path from the anode to the cathode, the electron separates from the lithium and travels to the other terminal. At the same time, the lithium becomes a +1 positively-charged ion and leaves the graphite lattice. When a lot of lithium ions leave at the same time, a flow of electrons is produced, travelling through and powering the device's circuits and components on their way to the cathode.
### The Cathode
In batteries used in personal consumer electronics, like smartphones, tablets, and cameras, limited space is available. **Lithium Cobalt Oxide** is used for its high specific energy, meaning it can store a lot of power while remaining small and lightweight. The tradeoff is that it has a relatively shorter lifespan, lower thermal stability, and is more expensive when compared to other cathode materials.

![[Pasted image 20260525115606.png]]
In the cathode is a lattice of cobalt and oxygen. The cobalt, having lost its electrons to the oxygen, are positively +4 charged, and are very willing to accept electrons. Since lithium wants to lose electrons, electrons from the anode travel to the cathode. As more and more electrons flow in one direction, the cathode begins to become more negatively-charged, while the anode becomes more and more positively-charged, which would stifle the flow of electrons in the right direction. To rectify this, an **electrolyte** provides a path for the now positive lithium ions to migrate from the anode to the cathode, while preventing the flow of electrons through it. When it reaches the cathode, lithium intercalates with the cobalt and oxygen to become Lithium Cobalt Oxide. The addition of positive lithium ions balances the charge. The process is not perfect, however, and even when the battery is fully charged, there will always be residual lithium ions present in the cathode, electrolyte, and anode.
### Additional Layers
![[Pasted image 20260525122958.png]]
A non-conductive **semipermeable separator** is placed between the anode and cathode; if the two were to make contact, with lithium still in the graphite, the reaction would accelerate at an uncontrollable rate and cause a fire or small explosion. The separator is percolated and allows lithium-ions to pass through; the electrolyte, being a liquid, isn't an effective barrier on its own.

![[Pasted image 20260525123753.png]]
Because the cobalt and graphite storing the lithium are relatively poor conductors, they need to be coated onto highly conductive metal foils. At the anode is a **copper collector** (copper is stable at low voltages, which the anode operates at), and at the cathode is an **aluminum collector** (aluminum is stable at high voltages and is lightweight and inexpensive). These metal sheets serve as the bridge between the internal battery chemistry and the external circuit. They collect the electrons generated during discharge and deliver them to the device, or accept electrons from a charger to feed back into the battery.

![[Pasted image 20260525124744.png]]
To maximize battery capacity while minimizing its size to fit in a smartphone, all of the layers are folded and wrapped into a rectangular prism package.

Additional circuitry is placed on the top of the battery to regulate the flow of electricity and prevent overcharging and damage to the battery.
### Recharging
At the end of the battery's lifespan the electrons and lithium from the anode will have all moved to the other end, and so the battery's charge will be empty. 

![[Pasted image 20260525115931.png]]When you plug in your device with a USB charger, it applies a higher force on the electrons in the opposite direction. The electrons leave the cobalt, restoring them to their positive state and repulsing the positive lithium ions. In the anode, the electrons are forced onto the graphite, which pulls the lithium ions to the other side through the electrolyte. The reaction is a reverse of the reaction that occurs when the battery is in use.
### Capacity Reduction Over Time
There are several reasons for why a battery's charge capacity gradually reduces over its lifetime. 
1. Sometimes lithium and electrons react with the electrolyte and organic solvent to form **solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)**. This is an irreversible process that permanently consumes lithium.
2. If the battery is completely discharged until it is empty, too much lithium can build up in the cathode, leading to a reaction that generates Lithium Oxide and Cobalt (II) Oxide. This is also irreversible, reducing the amount of lithium available.

Preventing the device's battery from completely draining can slow the battery's capacity reduction.
