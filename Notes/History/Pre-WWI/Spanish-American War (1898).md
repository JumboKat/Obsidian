### The Cuban Crisis
By the 19th century, the Spanish Empire was one in decline. In the 1810s, most of the former Spanish colonies in the Americas had gained their independence, with one exception being the island of Cuba. The United States had great interest in bringing Cuba under its influence, and worried that another European power would sweep in and seize it from the weakened Spanish. Americans, especially sugar and cotton plantation owners, also saw economic opportunity in Cuba. So, in 1823, U.S. President **James Monroe** announced the **Monroe Doctrine**, a pledge to keep the Americas free from foreign influences. 

![[Pasted image 20260603135451.png]]
Over the coming decades, the Cubans would rebel against Spanish rule in several revolutions, with tens of thousands of Spanish troops sent to suppress them. Tensions rose as the U.S. grew more and more interested in Cuba. The Americans made several offers to buy the territory, and portrayed Spain and backward and cruel in news publications and school textbooks. In 1873, the Spanish executed 58 sailors, including Americans, accused of gunrunning in support of the Cuban revolutionaries. 

In 1895, the Cuban Revolutionary Party, which started in the U.S., began another uprising. Under the leadership of Jose Marti, Maximo Gomez, and Antonio Maceo, they were unhappy with Spanish corruption, economic downturn (partially caused by U.S. tariffs on sugar) and the suspension of civil liberties. They employed scorched earth and guerilla tactics, burning down plantations and ambushing Spanish troops. In response, General **Valeriano Weyler** began a policy of reconcentration, leading to the deaths of 100,000 Cubans in squalid and disease-ridden camps. The U.S. tabloid "Yellow Press" media covered the atrocities in great detail, though faced criticism for exaggerating Spanish actions.

The American held a great distaste for the Spanish and called for action, though Washington was hesitant. In 1897, the newly-elected President **William McKinley** sought for a diplomatic solution. That same year, a more liberal government came to power in Madrid, who recalled General Weyler and promised reforms in response to critics in Spain and in the U.S. These promised reforms were too little, too late for the Cuban revolutionaries, who made demands for full independence. The situation reached a climax in February of 1898, when newspapers leaked a memo from the Spanish ambassador to the U.S. containing a fiery critique of President McKinley. Even worse, the USS Maine was sunk in Havana harbour, resulting in the deaths of 269 Americans. While it was most likely an internal failure, many Americans were quick to blame the Spanish. McKinley still wanted to avoid war, but was now facing mounting pressure to take action. Assistant Secretary of the Navy and future president **Theodore Roosevelt** famously criticized his indecision, stating: "McKinley has the backbone of a chocolate eclair!" 

Now facing potential mutiny from within his own republican party, he finally caved on April 11, requesting Congress for authorization to intervene and sending Spain an ultimatum demanding it withdraw its troops from Cuba and relinquish all claims of sovereignty over it. The Spanish agreed to some terms of the ultimatum, but refused on the issue of Cuban independence. On April 21st, the U.S. began blockading Cuba, prompting the Spanish to declare war on April 23. Two days later, the U.S. responded with its own declaration of war.
### Preparing for War
It was at this point the U.S. realized it was not fully prepared for this war. What was to be achieved from the war was still undecided. Eventually, Senator **Henry M. Teller** demanded an amendment denying any U.S. intention to annex Cuba which was approved by Congress with little opposition, angering expansionists but satisfying anti-imperialists. 

![[Pasted image 20260603143530.png]]
Militarily, the Americans were unprepared for war with Spain. In 1898, the U.S. Army was made up of just 28,000 men, and the militias, while larger, were not fit to fight overseas. So, the government announced a huge increase in military spending and made a call for volunteers. While the War Department were seeking 125,000 troops, they had 290,000 volunteers by August, one of whom was Theodore Roosevelt himself. He stepped down from his position in the navy to join the First Volunteer Cavalry division known as the **Rough Riders**. 

The army was not prepared for the influx of volunteers; training camps became overcrowded, with diseases like typhus and scarlet fever becoming widespread. Proper equipment was also a scarcity. Many were still armed with the outdated M1873 Springfield, which fired black powder cartridges. A lack of tropical equipment meant that some soldiers went in wearing woolen winter uniforms. The Spanish had 130,000-160,000 soldiers with varying degrees of morale and training, though all were well-equipped with the M1893 Mauser rifle and smokeless ammunition, and were more experienced in the Cuban conditions. As the armies of both sides trained and prepared, Spanish Admiral **Pascual Cervera y Topete** began his voyage from Cadiz to relieve the blockade of Cuba. Though Spain had historically held a strong navy, the U.S. Navy had seen a rapid expansion and modernization in recent years, and Cervera was pessimistic about the war's outcome for Spain.
### Battle of Manila Bay
While the war had been declared over tensions concerning Cuba, the first battles of the war would actually occur in the Philippines. Having been a Spanish colony since the 1570s, the islands had seen many uprisings in the 19th century, and a growing independence movement known as **La Liga Filipina**. While the U.S. initially had no interests in the Philippines, the Spanish Asiatic Fleet in Manila harbour was a potential threat to the U.S. Even before the declaration of war, Commodore George Dewey's U.S. Asiatic Fleet began preparing for operations and reaching out to potential allies among the repressed Filipinos. In 1896, **Emilio Aguinaldo** led mostly Tagalog-speaking Filipinos in a revolt in Luzon, prompting the Spanish to negotiate a deal: Aguinaldo would go into exile in exchange for $850,000 and promises of reform. By 1898, only half of the money was paid and no reforms were implemented, and so he continued to influence resistance in the Philippines from abroad. Now, he agreed to help the U.S. in an invasion of the islands under the guise of a promised Filipino Republic after victory, though the Americans later stated that they made no such promise.

![[Pasted image 20260604132016.png]]
Regardless, by late April 1898, Aguinaldo was contacting his fighters for a renewed uprising. Meanwhile, Dewey moved in on the fleet at Manila under **Patricio Montojo**, whose fleet was lacking, with just two protected cruisers, five unprotected cruisers, and five gunboats. Additionally, his mines were out of commission, and the American guns outranged his own. 

![[Pasted image 20260604132308.png]]
Expecting total defeat, Montojo positioned his ships to fight in shallow water, so that his sailors could avoid drowning when their ships sank. At 5:40 in the morning, the American ships engaged the Spanish in the harbour, making five rounds of attack. The battle was over by mid-morning, with 381 Spanish killed or wounded, while some claims suggest only one American died from a heart attack. Dewey's victory, while one-sided, was not enough for them to occupy Manila. By mid-May, the Americans were preparing an invasion force in San Francisco, but by then, British, French, German, and Japanese ships had already arrived to observe the situation and potentially take advantage of the chaos.
### U.S. Landings in Cuba
![[Pasted image 20260604133656.png]]By May 19th, Admiral Cervera was able to bypass the blockade and enter **Santiago de Cuba Bay**, but was then trapped there by Rear Admiral **William T. Sampson**. Now with no obstacles to an invasion, the U.S. focused its attention to the Cuban southeast coast, between Daiquiri and Siboney, sidestepping the bulk of Spanish troops concentrated around Havana. There, under the command of U.S. General **William Rufus Shafter** (who was 140kg and suffered from gout, but had experience in the Civil War as well as wars against Native Americans), they landed uncontested and linked up with Cuban rebel commander **Calixto García**. Relations between them soured quickly as the Cuban's strength and capabilities fell short of American expectations. The Cubans were only effective as a guerilla force skilled in ambushes, but the Americans thought of them as cowards for not engaging the Spaniards head-on in pitched battle. 

![[Pasted image 20260604163533.png]]
Shortly after landing, on June 24th Shafter's troops marched toward the **Las Guasimas** village, with the infantry taking the road and the Rough Riders following a route through the jungle. Despite the Spaniards' superior firearms, they were forced to withdraw after two hours of fighting.
### Battle of San Juan Hill
![[Pasted image 20260604164534.png]]
The Americans were now looking to take Santiago de Cuba, but the commanders were divided on their strategy. Admiral Sampson wanted Shafter to take out the coastal batteries defending the bay's entrance and clear the way for the navy, but Shafter instead decided to take the city himself; disease had begun to spread among his troops, and so he favoured speed. The Spaniards had set up defensive positions blocking the way to Santiago at El Caney and at San Juan and Kettle Hills. The Americans would attack the Spanish on two fronts; one force would attack at El Caney and dislodge them in a quick decisive victory, while another would pin the Spanish at San Juan and Kettle Hills to prevent them from supporting El Caney.

![[Pasted image 20260604165147.png]]
On July 1st, the Americans began their attack at 6:30 AM, but had underestimated the Spanish defenses, who held out for more than eight hours despite being outnumbered 10 to 1. With their artillery made obsolete, the Americans managed to break through using relatively novel hand-cranked **gatling guns**, whose automatic suppressive fire allowed infantry to close the distance. They cleared the barbed wire, breached the trenches, and flushed the Spanish out in close-quarters fighting. Kettle Hill was the first to fall, with San Juan following shortly after. After a failed counterattack, the surviving Spanish troops retreated to Santiago, with the position at El Caney also becoming untenable. The outcome of the battle had solidified Roosevelt as a war hero. Admiral Cervera, now fearing attack from both land and sea, attempted to escape the harbour, but was caught and destroyed by the American fleet. Santiago surrendered on the 17th, but the Cubans were banned from attending the surrender ceremony at the request of the Spanish. The fall of Santiago marked the end of the fighting in Cuba; 385 Americans and 800 Spanish died in combat, while 5,000 more Americans would succumb to disease.
### Puerto Rico Campaign
Even before the war, the U.S. placed diplomatic and economic pressure on the Spanish to increase Puerto Rico's autonomy. The island held strategic value, as it could be used as a military base in the Caribbean and to protect the Panama Canal. The people of Puerto Rico were divided between those who wanted autonomy under Spanish rule, outright independence, or annexation by the U.S. 

![[Pasted image 20260604170521.png]]
The U.S. Navy had been blockading the capital of San Juan since May, and on July 25th, troops landed at the town of **Guánica**, facing minimal resistance. The war in Puerto Rico was limited to just light skirmishes and ended after 19 days with the ceasefire on August 13, with only 3 Americans and 17 Spanish soldiers killed. The U.S. had taken full control of the island, with mixed reception from the locals. Many peasants took the opportunity to loot and burn plantations, even going so far as to murder the owners of plantations whom they saw as oppressive.
### End of the War in the Philippines

![[Pasted image 20260604171123.png]]
In late June, the U.S. had captured the island of **Guam** from the Spanish, before landing at **Cavite** in the Philippines on July 1st. While the local Spaniards tried to rally Filipino troops under their cause, most were loyal to Aguinaldo's resistance, and by the time the Americans had arrived, Aguinaldo's forces had already surrounded Manila. The Filipinos were concerned by the calls of U.S. politicians and businessmen to annex the Philippines, and so Aguinaldo, against the advice of the Americans, declared Filipino independence on June 18th.

As relations between the U.S. and the Filipino resistance worsened, U.S. General **Wesley Merrit** proceeded with discussing surrender with the Spanish without the support of Aguinaldo. Spanish governor-general **Fermín Jáudenes y Álvarez** was willing to surrender to the Americans but feared the Spanish penalty for not contesting the city, and so both sides agreed to a staged battle on August 13th, though in some places, the fight was real and produced casualties. Even this staged battle was unnecessary, as both nations had already agreed to a ceasefire in Washington by this point.
### Treaty of Paris
On December 10th, 1898, a peace treaty was signed in Paris, with the agreed upon terms as follows:
- Spain was to cede Guam and Puerto Rico to the United States
- Cuba was to become independent, with a U.S. military administration overseeing it for some time
- The U.S. would purchase the Philippines from Spain for $20 million (over $700 million today), with Spanish administration temporarily staying in place

McKinley claimed that U.S. control would introduce infrastructure and peace to the islands, although commercial interests, fears of foreign takeover by other nations, and religious convictions more likely influenced the decision.
### Aftermath
With the Cubans and Filipinos not consulted in the terms of the treaty, the Filipinos took action. On January 23, 1899, Aguinaldo approved the constitution for a Philippine Republic, and uprisings began on February 4th. Lacking the ability to match the U.S. forces strength-to-strength, the Filipino resistance fighters retreated into the mountains, from where they would wage a guerilla war. By the summer, 35,000 American soldiers were stationed in the Philippines attempting to suppress the rebellion. By June 1901, one thousand attacks had been launched against U.S. troops. The American response was one of brutality; civilians in Luzon were forced into concentration camps and arrested and executed Filipino men of military age. In March 1901, Aguinaldo was captured. His movement was defeated, having lost the people's support after having executed  rival resistance leaders. He took an oath of allegiance to the United States and disbanded the Philippine republic, though resistance from republican and Muslim Filipinos would continue. In the end, 5,000 U.S. troops, 20,000 Filipino revolutionaries, and 250,000 civilians, mostly from disease and starvation, had died as a result of the conflict.

Having been short and one-sided, the war marked the end of Spain's once-global empire. In 1899, Spain sold its remaining holdings in the Pacific to Germany. Liberal thinkers called for reforms in Spain, while military leaders favoured more conservative approaches. The U.S. now held its own overseas empire, it felt more confident in its place on the world stage and was free to influence the Americas as it wished, with American companies like **Bethlehem Steel** and the **United Fruit Company** seeking potential for new profits.
