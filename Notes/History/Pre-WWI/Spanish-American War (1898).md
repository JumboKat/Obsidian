### The Cuban Crisis
By the 19th century, the Spanish Empire was one in decline. In the 1810s, most of the former Spanish colonies in the Americas had gained their independence, with one exception being the island of Cuba. The United States had great interest in bringing Cuba under its influence, and worried that another European power would sweep in and seize it from the weakened Spanish. Americans, especially sugar and cotton plantation owners, also saw economic opportunity in Cuba. So, in 1823, U.S. President **James Monroe** announced the **Monroe Doctrine**, a pledge to keep the Americas free from foreign influences.

Over the coming decades, the Cubans would rebel against Spanish rule in several revolutions, with tens of thousands of Spanish troops sent to suppress them. Tensions rose as the U.S. grew more and more interested in Cuba. The Americans made several offers to buy the territory, and portrayed Spain and backward and cruel in news publications and school textbooks. In 1873, the Spanish executed 58 sailors, including Americans, accused of gunrunning in support of the Cuban revolutionaries. 

In 1895, the Cuban Revolutionary Party, which started in the U.S., began another uprising. Under the leadership of Jose Marti, Maximo Gomez, and Antonio Maceo, they were unhappy with Spanish corruption, economic downturn (partially caused by U.S. tariffs on sugar) and the suspension of civil liberties. They employed scorched earth and guerilla tactics, burning down plantations and ambushing Spanish troops. In response, General **Valeriano Weyler** began a policy of reconcentration, leading to the deaths of 100,000 Cubans in squalid and disease-ridden camps. The U.S. tabloid "Yellow Press" media covered the atrocities in great detail, though faced criticism for exaggerating Spanish actions.

The American held a great distaste for the Spanish and called for action, though Washington was hesitant. In 1897, the newly-elected President **William McKinley** sought for a diplomatic solution. That same year, a more liberal government came to power in Madrid, who recalled General Weyler and promised reforms in response to critics in Spain and in the U.S. These promised reforms were too little, too late for the Cuban revolutionaries, who made demands for full independence. The situation reached a climax in February of 1898, when newspapers leaked a memo from the Spanish ambassador to the U.S. containing a fiery critique of President McKinley. Even worse, the USS Maine was sunk in Havana harbour, resulting in the deaths of 269 Americans. While it was most likely an internal failure, many Americans were quick to blame the Spanish. McKinley still wanted to avoid war, but was now facing mounting pressure to take action. Assistant Secretary of the Navy and future president **Theodore Roosevelt** famously criticized his indecision, stating: "McKinley has the backbone of a chocolate eclair!" 

Now facing potential mutiny from within his own republican party, he finally caved on April 11, requesting Congress for authorization to intervene and sending Spain an ultimatum demanding it withdraw its troops from Cuba and relinquish all claims of sovereignty over it. The Spanish agreed to some terms of the ultimatum, but refused on the issue of Cuban independence. On April 21st, the U.S. began blockading Cuba, prompting the Spanish to declare war on April 23. Two days later, the U.S. responded with its own declaration of war.
### Preparing for War
It was at this point the U.S. realized it was not fully prepared for this war. What was to be achieved from the war was still undecided. Eventually, Senator **Henry M. Teller** demanded an amendment denying any U.S. intention to annex Cuba which was approved by Congress with little opposition, angering expansionists but satisfying anti-imperialists.

Militarily, the Americans were unprepared for war with Spain. In 1898, the U.S. Army was made up of just 28,000 men, and the militias, while larger, were not fit to fight overseas. So, the government announced a huge increase in military spending and made a call for volunteers. While the War Department were seeking 125,000 troops, they had 290,000 volunteers by August, one of whom was Theodore Roosevelt himself. He stepped down from his position in the navy to join the First Volunteer Cavalry division known as the **Rough Riders**. 

The army was not prepared for the influx of volunteers; training camps became overcrowded, with diseases like typhus and scarlet fever becoming widespread. Proper equipment was also a scarcity. Many were still armed with the outdated M1873 Springfield, which fired black powder cartridges. A lack of tropical equipment meant that some soldiers went in wearing woolen winter uniforms. The Spanish had 130,000-160,000 soldiers with varying degrees of morale and training, though all were well-equipped with the M1893 Mauser rifle and smokeless ammunition, and were more experienced in the Cuban conditions.
