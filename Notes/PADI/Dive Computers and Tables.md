To avoid decompression sickness (DCS), you must keep body nitrogen levels within accepted limits. However, as there is no way to directly monitor your body's nitrogen levels, we use dive computers and tables as a reference.
### How They Work
Dive computers and tables use your dive time and depth to calculate the theoretical nitrogen content of your body. These estimates are compared against limits based on experimental dives and human experience. 

**Dive computers** apply a decompression model to depth and time information. The computer constantly updates your calculated nitrogen content and compares it to the model. Before dive computers, divers relied on **dive tables**. With the **Recreational Dive Planner (RDP) Table**, you use the depth from a depth gauge and the time from a timer or watch to look up the accepted limits on the RDP Table. An eRDP is an electronic calculator that takes depth and time information as inputs and looks it up on the table for you.

Because dive computers do not make direct assessments (only projections based on models), and people can vary in their susceptibility to decompression sickness, no dive computer or table can guarantee that it will never occur. You must accept that there is always some risk of DCS when you go diving. Dive within the limits of your dive computer or table. Ascend slowly (18m/60ft per minute or slower as required by your computer) and make a three minute safety stop at 5m/15ft.
### No Stop Diving
Also known as "no decompression diving," **no stop diving** is a swim directly to the surface without unacceptable risk of DCS. You plan your dives so that they are always well within no stop limits. A **no stop limit** is the maximum time you can spend at a given depth and still ascend directly to the surface. Exceeding a no stop limit means you must make one or more emergency decompression stops, which occur at specific depths at prescribed times to allow your body to released dissolved nitrogen before ascending further. The dive computer can show the no stop limits for any given depth.

Note that the no stop time decreases significantly faster as you go deeper. You also use air faster as you go deeper. Thus, the deeper you go, the more frequently you should check your remaining air supply and no stop time.

During the dive, the computer will constantly update your remaining no stop time. The times displayed in the table are only for if you would have stayed at the depth the entire dive (which is uncommon). One advantage of dive computers is the ability to recalculate no stop dive time as you ascend, which is called **mulitlevel diving**. With dive tables, you must treat the dive as if you spend its entirety at its deepest depth. An eRDP allows you to plan multilevel times to increase your stop time as you ascend, but it is not as versatile.
#### Ascent Rates
Most computers and dive tables have a required ascent rate which the no stop time assumes. Going faster than this rate will increase your risk of DCS and lung overexpansion injury. Most computers will have audio or visual warnings if you ascend too fast.
### Repetitive Diving
Repetitive diving, that is, making dives within a short time frame of each other, causes subsequent dives to have harsher no stop limits due to residual nitrogen dissolved in your body after a dive. It can take hours after surfacing for all your excess nitrogen to dissolve, up to even longer than a day. Making a dive while you still have residual nitrogen is called a **repetitive dive**. Staying at the surface long enough (12+ hours) for your nitrogen levels to normalize means your next dive will not be a repetitive dive, but rather a "clean" dive. A **surface interval** is the time you spend at the surface in between dives. The longer the interval, the more stop time you have on a repetitive dive.

Following a dive, your computer continues to calculate after you've surfaced, still retaining your dissolved nitrogen information. Thus, if you make a repetitive dive, your computer still knows how much residual nitrogen is left in your body, and will calculate no stop times accordingly. This also means that you should use the **same computer the entire diving day** on all dives and not share it with another diver. Also, do not turn it off, as you risk losing your dive information; most modern computers won't let you turn them off for this reason. They may go to 'sleep' to retain power, but will still remain calculating. They also will not let you dive if the battery power is too low. Once it calculates that nitrogen levels are normal, it will turn off/go to sleep, though many, like watches, never turn off or go to sleep.

The RDP and other dive tables account for repetitive diving by using three tables. The first assigns a pressure group (letter) representing the theoretical amount of residual nitrogen from your dive time and depth. The second gives you credit for nitrogen leaving your body during a surface interval. Taking the pressure group from the previous table, it assigns a new pressure group based on your surface interval time.

### Advantages of Dive Computers
Dive computers are superior to dive tables as they offer four advantages:
1. Dive computers are easier to use than tables.
2. Dive computers help offset human error.
3. Dive computers give you more time underwater.
4. Dive computers have additional features, like recording water temperature, and an eLog.
#### Dive Computer Information
All dive computers provide the following information before, during, and/or between dives:
- No stop (no decompression) limits
- Depth
- Elapsed time underwater and between dives
- Remaining no stop time
- Ascent rate, including visual speedometers and alarms
- Emergency decompression
- Previous dive information
### Dive Computer Failure
Dive computers are extremely reliable and failure is very rare. Most computers confirm adequate battery power for 12-24 hours when you turn them on. Failures can result in bad or no information being displayed. If a failure occurs, it rarely fixes itself; have it serviced before using it again even if it seems fine. If a failure occurs during a dive, there are two options:
1. Signal your buddies and end the dive, making a safety stop along the way.
2. Continue using a backup computer. If you are using a backup, you must dive with both computers at all times to ensure both have the correct information. If both were to fail, defer to option 1.
If failure occurs between dives, you may be able to use a table. Otherwise, wait 12 hours at the surface for your nitrogen levels to normalize.
