
### BCD - Buoyancy Control Device
The BCD is comprised of several components that best function as a single unit, which are:
- **Inflatable bladder**: a very durable bag that you inflate or deflate to change your buoyancy
- **Cylinder band and harness/jacket**: the bladder integrates with an adjustable harness that holds the cylinder on your back. The bladder can be entirely behind you or partially wrap around your waist and/or over your shoulders
- **Low pressure inflator (LPI) mechanism**: Inflates the bladder with air from your cylinder via the regulator when you press a button. Another button triggers deflation of the bladder, or to inflate it orally. Usually at the end of a large-diameter hose.
- **Overpressure/quick exhaust valves**: An overpressure valve that automatically vents air if the BCD is too full, to prevent rupturing of the bladder. Some have 'quick dump' valves to manually release air, which can be easier than using the LPI exhaust depending on your position in the water.
- **Weight system**: Many BCDs have weight pockets that can be released in an emergency.

When choosing a BCD, consider these options:
- **Bladder capacity**: BCDs cover a wide range of diving circumstances, and should have ample buoyancy to float you and all your equipment to the surface. This is sometimes called 'lift' capacity.
- **Pockets**: for storage
- **D-rings**: for attaching accessories
- **Shoulder quick release**: a quick-release buckle on one or both shoulders to make it easy to remove the kit.

In addition to regular maintenance, there are two things that must be done with BCDs:
1. Rinse the bladder with fresh water: fill it about 1/3rd full with water, slosh it around, and then drain it through the LPI exhaust and quick dumps.
2. Store the BCD partially inflated to prevent the bladder from sticking together inside.

### Regulator
![[Pasted image 20260520095614.png]]
The **regulator** regulates air flow from the cylinder and consists of 5 components:
1. First stage; supplies air to the components
2. Second stage
3. Alternate air source
4. Low-pressure inflator hose
5. SPG/Computer

The **first stage** is the 'hub' of the regulator. It supplies air to all other components. It connects to the cylinder valve either by screwing into it or with a yoke (clamp system). An o-ring forms the air-tight seal between the first stage and the cylinder valve. The first stage reduces cylinder pressure to an intermediate pressure, which is 7-10 bar/100-150 psi above surrounding pressure.

The **second stage** is what you breathe from. It reduces the intermediate pressure from the first stage to the pressure around you. It delivers air only when you exhale and has one-way valves that vent your exhalation. It includes a purge button that manually releases air from the cylinder.

![[Pasted image 20260520100247.png]]
When you inhale, the diaphragm is pulled inward. This depresses the air valve, releasing air from the first stage. 
![[Pasted image 20260520100354.png]]
When you exhale, the diaphragm relaxes, lifting the lever and closing the valve to the first stage. The exhaled air is vented via one-way exhaust valves.

The **alternate air source** or 'octopus' is an extra second stage used for sharing air with someone else if needed. An **alternate inflator regulator** can also be used, which combines a second stage with the BCD low-pressure inflator. **Pony bottles** and **self-contained ascent bottles**. are small cylinders with their own regulators. They can be shared or used by you. A self-contained ascent bottle has just enough air to reach the surface and usually attaches to the BCD harness. Pony bottles are larger and hold more air, and are typically strapped to the main cylinder or attached to D-rings on the BCD.

The alternate air source second stage attaches with a quick release. It should not be left to dangle, as it can fill with mud or sand and get damaged.

The **low-pressure inflator (LPI)** hose supplies air to the BCD inflator. When diving in a dry suit, a second LPI is used to add air to the suit during descent.

The **Submersible Pressure Gauge (SPG)** displays the remaining air pressure in the cylinder. In its simplest form, it is a hose with a mechanical gauge. It can also be built into the dive computer/air-integrated computer. The SPG typically secures under your left arm and on the lower-to-mid left torso. It should not be left to dangle, as it can get damaged/damage the environment, and creates drag. 
#### Regulator Considerations
Consider the following when choosing a regulator:
- **Ease of breathing**: higher-end models usually breathe the easiest, though all regulators from reputable manufacturers will meet breathing requirements for normal recreational limits.
- **Yoke or DIN**: The yoke system holds the first stage to the cylinder via clamps. In the DIN system, the regulator threads into the valve. It is common to choose a DIN regulator with a yoke adapter, which is usable on either type of valve.
- **Adjustable second stage**: A knob that allows small airflow adjustments.
- **Dive/predive switch**: reduces freeflow (air released without control) when the second stage isn't in your mouth.
- **Cold-water first stage**: The first stage can freeze in cooler temperatures, resulting in freeflow. The first stage can be surrounded with a special liquid to prevent this.
#### Maintenance
When rinsing, use a gentle fresh water flow with the first stage dust cap tightly in place, or with the first stage attached to a cylinder. Do not let water enter the air inlet where it meets the cylinder second valve.

Run water through the second stages while rinsing, but do not press the purge button, as it could allow water to flow up the hose to the first stage.

Regulators require periodic overhauls by a professional every 1-2 years.

### Cylinder
The cylinder or tank is an aluminum or steel alloy container that stores high pressure air. It is comprised of a cylinder and a valve.

The valve controls air flow to-and-from the cylinder. It is made of chrome-plated brass, and, in many countries, has a burst disk, a safety device that relieves overpressure by rupturing and releasing the air well before the cylinder would fail.
