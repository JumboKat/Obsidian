A capacitor stores electric charge like a battery, aside from a few key differences:
- It stores charge using an electric field (rather than chemically).
- It's capacity is much smaller than a battery's.
- It can charge/discharge much faster than a battery can.
Instead, a capacitor is more similar to a water tower, in that it provides temporary storage to provide a continued outflow even when the source inflow has halted. As long as the capacitor is not completely empty, it will provide an uninterrupted flow of electrons, smoothing out interruptions in supply; brief interruptions will have no effect on the output devices in an electrical circuit.
### Inside a Capacitor
![[Pasted image 20260723165326.png]]
![[Pasted image 20260723165347.png]]
A typical capacitor has two terminals, each one separately connecting to the anode and cathode (often sheets of aluminum), which are separated by a dielectric (usually made of ceramic). A dielectric is a poor conductor that can polarize when exposed to an electric field. Normally, the charges within a dielectric are scattered and randomly distributed, cancelling each other out. When voltage is applied, the charges are pulled in opposite directions.

![[Pasted image 20260723163346.png|300]]
Capacitors are usually detailed with a stripe to indicate where the negative terminal is.

![[Pasted image 20260723165714.png]]If a capacitor is connected to a voltage source (like a battery), the electrons will flow from the negative terminal to the capacitor. They build up on one plate, while they are released from the other. Because of the insulator, the electrons cannot pass through the capacitor. Eventually, the voltage of the capacitor and the battery will be equal, so no more electrons will flow. With a build up of electrons on one side, there is now a voltage difference between the two sides of the capacitor, with a positively-charged side and negatively-charged side. The electrons on the negative side are pulled toward the positive side (establishing an electric field), but cannot directly reach it due to the insulator.

![[Pasted image 20260723164058.png]]
If a path is created connecting the two sides, electrons will flow through it, powering any devices along the way. This will continue until the buildup of electrons is equal on both sides, where the voltage is zero. When the power source is connected again, the capacitor will begin 'charging'; electrons will flow from one end of the capacitor to the power supply, and from the power supply to the other end of the capacitor.

On the side of capacitors can be found the labels for capacitance (in Farads, usually microFarads), and voltage (in Volts), which is the maximum voltage rating for the capacitor; exceeding this voltage can result in the capacitor exploding.
### Application of Capacitors
Capacitors are mostly known for their use in circuit boards, but larger versions are also used in AC units, ceiling fans, and induction motors. Even larger ones are used in **capacitor banks** for correct poor power factor in large buildings. When too many loads are placed on a circuit, the current can start to lag behind the voltage. A capacitor bank is used to rectify this.

![[Pasted image 20260723165205.png]]
Capacitors are also used to smooth out peaks when converting AC to DC power. A **full bridge rectifier** is used to flip the negative portion of an AC sine wave, making its output appear as DC, though there will still be gaps between the peaks. A capacitor can be used to release energy during the gaps to smooth it out.
