### Prelude
After its reunification in in 1871, Italy experienced a continued wave of nationalism and expansionism, with hopes of establishing a new "Roman Empire" via imperial conquest in the Mediterranean. 

![[Pasted image 20260523144000.png]]
However, Italian hopes were dashed as great powers made their claim on North Africa; France had occupied Algeria and Tunisia, and Britain controlled Egypt. To the Italians, the Ottoman-controlled provinces of Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, Fezzan (today's Libya) remained the only viable target, and they worried that it would be taken by France or Britain if they did not act quickly. While Italy had been spreading its influence in the region by establishing banks, schools, and hospitals, many politicians still called for military action.

The Ottomans, aware of Italy's ambitions, attempted to appease them via economic concessions, but the Ottoman Empire was now in a greatly weakened state, with internal issues brought to light by the Young Turks Revolution in 1908. Any concessions made would only bring temporary reprieve, and the Italians would never be permanently satisfied.

As tensions rose in 1911, Italian Prime Minister **Giovani Giolitti** went on a public campaign to win over nationalist support for military action, but was still cautiously aware of the strength of the Ottoman Empire. Despite his deliberation, he still felt squeezed on time. Alongside the concern of French or British intervention, Italy's allies opposed any decision to weaken the Ottomans; Austria-Hungary wanted stable Ottoman control over the Balkans, and Germany wanted a dependable ally against the Entente. 

That year, Italy and France struck a deal, agreeing to not interfere with each other's ambitions in North Africa. Meanwhile, the Ottomans actually moved troops away from Libya to deal with an uprising in Yemen, though they did arm the locals with weapons in anticipation of the war to come.
### Italy's Ultimatum and Declaration of War
On September 27th, 1911, Giolitti gave the Ottomans an ultimatum, citing supposed bias against Italian business interests in the region. The Ottomans were to allow Italian occupation of Libya within 24 hours or face military action. While the Ottomans offered concessions, these were rejected by the Italians, and as 24 hours had gone by, the Italian government declared war.

![[Pasted image 20260523150731.png]]
The Italian military, having not been notified on the government's full intentions until September, had to hastily organize a last-minute invasion. Between October 1st-23rd, Italian troops landed at Tripoli, Homs, Tobruk, Benghazi, and Derna, brushing aside Ottoman resistance with ease with superior firepower and numbers. Advancing into the countryside would prove much more difficult, however, as the Italians had very little knowledge of the land, and even relied on ancient mappings from Julius Caesar. They hoped that the Ottomans would surrender after taking the towns, but they simply retreated beyond the range of Italy's naval guns. Italian plans had also greatly misjudged the attitudes of the local population, believing to be welcomed by them as liberators. Instead, the native Arabs sided with the Ottomans in resisting the Italians.

![[Pasted image 20260524123154.png]]
The Italians came fitted with new equipment, such as their modern gray-green uniforms and the **Carcano**, both of which would see use until the end of World War Two. They were also supported by the large naval guns of their ships offshore, an advantage they held over the Ottomans. There was only a small force of 2500-5000 Ottoman regulars, with 35,000 Arab tribesmen making up the bulk of their numbers. The Italians overall were technologically and numerically superior. 
### Battle at Shar al-Shatt
The Italians had entrenched themselves around Tripoli, but their trenches ran directly through an oasis, which could provide cover for a potential Ottoman attack. 

![[Pasted image 20260524124654.png]]
On October 23rd, the Ottomans launched an attack over a six kilometre-wide front with a diversionary attack south of the town, with their target being the lesser-defended Shar al-Shatt. The local Saraceni also came out from the town of Shar al-Shatt and attacked the Italians from behind. Although they sustained heavy losses, the Italians were able to repel the attack with the arrival of reinforcements. The attack prompted the Italians to respond with a massacre of 4000 locals after the battle, as well as swelling their troop numbers to over 100,000, well above what was originally planned. Following this, on November 5th, Giolitti formally announced the full annexation of Libya, a mere symbolic gesture at the time since the Italians only controlled the coast, and ensured that the Turks would continue to fight. While they were victorious at Shar al-Shatt, it was a disappointment in the realization that the war would not be over as quick as they had initially hoped.
### The Trench Phase
Following the battle, the war entered a period of stalemate. While the Ottomans could not expel the Italians, the Italians struggled to completely defeat the Turks and Arabs, who were now resorting to guerilla warfare. Italy's naval supremacy was mostly preventing Ottoman reinforcements, though the Ottomans were able to sneak in small shipments of arms and  volunteer officers, including **Enver Bey** and **Mustafa Kemal**. 

The Ottoman strategy now relied on trapping the Italians at the coastal towns and sapping their strength through attrition. Italian commanders wanted to make a push through the desert, but lacked the intelligence, logistics, and proper desert equipment, and would face continued harassment by the guerillas. Thus, they made small advances at a time, digging trenches as they went, sometimes as often as 100 metres.

![[Pasted image 20260524172413.png]]
On December 4th, the Italians attempted to bring the Ottomans to a decisive battle at **Ain Zara**, an Ottoman-controlled base on the high ground overlooking Tripoli. With the support of heavy artillery and naval guns, the Italians forced the Ottomans out from their entrenched positions, though they failed to surround them, allowing most of the Ottomans to escape. 

The victory at Ain Zara was not the decisive victory the Italians were looking for. As the war dragged on, the Ottomans were gaining confidence. Time was on their side, and there was a vast amount of desert for them to fall back to if need be. Italian morale declined as disease ran rampant. Despite this, Italian media coverage did not wane, and most interest was held by the war in the air.
### The War in the Air
The Italo-Turkish War was the first conflict to see significant use of airplanes for reconnaissance and bombings. On October 23rd, an Italian reconnaissance mission along the coast marked the first ever official combat flight; on October 25th, the Ottomans were the first to hit enemy aircraft with anti-aircraft fire, and on November 1st, the Italians made the first ever bombing raid. 

While public attention was held captive by the spectacle of aerial bombing, its impact on the war was marginal. Reconnaissance in the air proved the most valuable to the Italian war effort. Overall, the Italians had proven that aircraft were fit for military use.
### The War at Sea
As the war continued into 1912, the Italians turned to the navy to deliver the knockout blow to the Ottomans at sea. 

![[Pasted image 20260524174646.png]]
In January of 1912, the Italian navy sunk several Ottoman ships in the **Red Sea** and the **Gulf of Aden**, and transported weapons to rebel groups in Ottoman-controlled Yemen and Arabia. This was followed by an Italian naval victory at Beirut Harbour in February. 

In April, the Italians sent a flotilla to the **Dardanelles Strait**, a vital international shipping lane that gave access to the Ottoman capital of **Constantinople**. Following a series of inconclusive skirmishes, the Ottomans closed and mined the strait in defense of their capital. This caught the attention of Great Britain and especially Russia, who depended on shipping through the Dardanelles. Pressure was put on both the Italians and Ottomans, though it was the latter who were forced to reopen the strait. Italy was also at odds with its ally Austria-Hungary, who did not want a weakened Ottoman Empire in fear of an unstable Balkans. Despite these diplomatic pressures against the Italians, the Ottomans were unable to capitalize on the situation, being too politically unstable at home. 

![[Pasted image 20260524180101.png]]
Despite the political risks, the Italians believed their naval strategy was the key to victory, even suspending ground operations in Libya. They now turned to the Ottoman-controlled **Rhodes** and **Dodecanese Islands**. Italian control of these islands would severely restrict Ottoman naval movements and serve as a bargaining chip when negotiating terms of peace. The islands were protected by a status quo agreement by the Triple Alliance, and it was only after Austria-Hungary permitted a temporary occupation that the Italians could proceed, a decision made under pressure by Germany who wanted to strengthen the alliance before it came up for renewal in 1912.
### Peace Talks
Between April 28th and May 21st of 1912, the Italians captured the thirteen islands in the **Aegean Sea**, facing very little resistance. The capture of these islands furthered divisions among the Ottomans between those who wanted to continue the war and those who wanted peace. The position of both parties was difficult; the Italians were reluctant to comprise, having already declared the full annexation of Libya. The Ottomans were not keen on giving concessions, since they hadn't faced total defeat and were still in a position to fight.

In May, Russian-led peace talks began, but failed. In June, another round of peace talks began in Switzerland, but they too, ultimately fell through; the Ottomans were willing to accept an independent Libya with Italian influence, but this was not enough for the Italians. However, anti-war sentiment was growing in Italy. The war was becoming unpopular at home, and soldiers in the trenches were deserting. The war made up 47% of Italy's total spending, turning even pro-war news publications against it. In a last attempt at gaining leverage against the Ottomans, the Italians sent camouflaged torpedo boats to sneak attack the Ottoman ships at Ankor, although they were spotted by sentries and sent away. 
### Treaty of Ouchy
In August, peace talks resumed. The new Ottoman government was now facing pressure to negotiate, partly by the other powers, and partly by the recent outbreak of the [[The Balkan Wars (1912-1913)|First Balkan War]] in early October. The Ottomans wanted to avoid giving the impression that they had abandoned the Libyan Arabs, which could potentially spark unrest in other Arab regions of the empire. On October 18th, 1912, the Italo-Turkish War ended with the signing of the **Treaty of Ouchy**. The outcome of the treaty was as follows:
- The Ottomans declared Libya as independent, avoiding accepting Italian sovereignty over it. However, they would not object when the Italians later declared sovereignty anyway.
- The Sultan would continue as the religious head of the Libyan Muslims.
- Italy was to return the Aegean islands back to the Ottomans.
- European powers recognized Italian control over Libya.
### Aftermath
While Italy had successfully wrested control of Libya from the Ottomans, the reaction at home was not overly positive. The war was costly in both treasure and blood, and did not bring the spectacle of victory they were promised. The victory also did little to improve the image of Italy's military in the eyes of its European contemporaries. The new colony of Libya was not easy to govern, either. The Italians would face insurrection by the locals for years to come, and would respond with brutal repression. Italy also refused to return the Aegean islands, citing the increased costs of Libyan occupation, though at this point the Ottomans had their hands tied by the [[The Balkan Wars (1912-1913)|First Balkan War]].

While the loss of Libya as their last possession in Africa contributed to the Ottoman Empire's reputation as the "Sick Man of Europe," they were now at least lifted of the economic burden of maintaining control of the territory. The suffering of the Libyan peoples could not be understated, however, and special refugee offices were set up in Constantinople for those fleeing Italian repression.

Like the Austrians had feared, the Italo-Turkish War helped destabilize the Balkans and fueled the outbreak of the [[The Balkan Wars (1912-1913)|Balkan Wars]], solidifying the conflict as one of many of the series of sparks that would ignite the fuse of The Great War to come.
