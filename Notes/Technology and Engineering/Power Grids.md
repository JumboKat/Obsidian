Electricity is a resource that is consumed at the same time it is produced. In North America, there are four distinct power grids that serve almost all of the continent: the **Western** and **Eastern** grids, and the separated power grids of Quebec and Texas.
### Three Phases of the Grid
![[Pasted image 20260626102919.png]]
A power grid, known in technical terms as a **wide area interconnection**, can be divided into three phases.

At generation, electricity is produced at a power plant, converting one form of energy into another. Most are located away from populated areas, and so rely on long-range [[Transmission Lines||transmission lines]]. At the power plant, a [[Substations#Transformation||transformer]] ramps up the voltage to increase its efficiency over long distances. Once it has been delivered to populated areas, another transformer at a [[Substations||substation]] reduces the voltage down to a level safe for consumption. At distribution, most customers are served electricity via **feeder lines** from substations, though some get their energy directly from transmission lines. From the feeders, smaller transformers perform the final step down of voltage for industrial, commercial, or residential use.

The vast majority of the power grid makes use of alternating current (AC), as it is easy to step voltages up or down in order to move it efficiently and safely.
### Benefits
#### Power Quality and Stability
Our electronics and equipment are designed with the assumption that incoming power is stable in both voltage and frequency. Some even count the oscillations of the alternating current to keep track of time. Changes in voltage can lead to brownouts (a partial, temporary power reduction) or even damage in electrical devices. To combat this, a large power grid maintains **electrical inertia**, created by the large spinning rotors of traditional power generators (like hydro, coal, gas). Acting like a flywheel, they resist fluctuations in frequency.
#### Reliability
To provide constant, available energy and avoid blackouts, redundancies are provided and faults are managed at [[Substations||substations]], where electricity can be rerouted in case an equipment experiences a fault or reaches the end of its planned life.
#### Supply and Demand
Electricity is supplied as it is demanded, meaning that the grid must be able to match fluctuations in demand with the appropriate level of energy generation. Because so many people are connected to one grid, the demand for electricity is smooth and generally predictable, following a trend throughout the day. However, external factors like extreme weather can be difficult to forecast demand. 

![[Pasted image 20260626105702.png]]
Grid operators dispatch energy generation in real time. The cheapest forms of energy are used to supply the base load (lowest level of demand), while pricier methods are used to meet demand as it rises. They must also contend with many different factors, such as:
- Startup times of large power plants (can take anywhere from hours to weeks)
- Equipment maintenance
- Fluctuating costs of fuels
- Variations in renewable sources like wind and solar
