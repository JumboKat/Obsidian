#infrastructure 

Rain in urban environments presents a challenge, as water does not easily percolate through the ground, but rather runs off of sideways, streets, and buildings, funneling into nearby creeks and rivers and swelling them with dirt and pollution (or if there are none, potentially flooding populated areas). Thus, most municipalities have design policies for rainwater drainage for buildings and infrastructure.
#### Street Drainage
![[Pasted image 20260714131055.png]]
Individual lots are typically on a slant, so that rainwater flows toward the street. The street itself has a crown at its centre and drains on either side, keeping the road dry.
### Storm Sewer Systems
Streets are only the first line of defense and must channel the water elsewhere. In the past, rainwater was simply directed to sewage systems. Sewage systems are not designed to handle spontaneous surges of rainwater, however, and would often have to dump untreated sewage directly into waterways. Thus, most cities use **Municipal Separate Storm Sewer Systems (MS4)**. They are networks of curbs, ditches, gutters, and sewer pipes specifically dedicated to directing rainwater runoff to natural waterways. They usually do not treat the water in any way, and so are not meant for dumping waste.
#### Channelization
![[Pasted image 20260714131807.png]]
Getting water out as fast as possible reduces the risk and depth of flooding, and so many cities have flat, wide open channels lined with smooth concrete, mostly free of vegetation to facilitate the flow of water. However, channelization has fallen out of favour; it is visually unappealing, it worsens the flooding downstream, and destroys the natural waterway. Simply widening channels to meet rainwater demands is not an efficient solution.
#### Stormwater Retention 
Many municipalities hold land developers responsible for their own increase in runoff, leading many to create on-site storage for rainwater. 
![[Pasted image 20260714132352.png]]Retention ponds act as temporary storage for water, retaining the peak of the runoff water while limiting the outflow to a rate equal to or less than what it was naturally before the ground was urbanized. This also reduces pollution, as slowing the rate of water allows suspended particles to settle out.
### Natural Solutions
Watersheds in nature don't just get rid of rainwater, but make use of it. They create ponds and habitats for wildlife, clean and filter rainwater via vegetation, divert rain into the ground to recharge underground aquifers, and reduce flooding by slowing the flow of water at the source.

![[Pasted image 20260714133212.png]]
Many cities are trying to replicate the functions of natural watersheds in order to reap its benefits and preserve natural water systems. In the U.S., this is known as **low impact development**, and includes solutions such as rain gardens, rooftop gardens, rainwater barrels, permeable pavement, etc.
