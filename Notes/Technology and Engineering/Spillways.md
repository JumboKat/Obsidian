### What is a Spillway?
Dams are built for the purpose of storing water and protecting against flooding caused by spikes in inflows. But even for dams, water surges can vary enormously and quickly (such as in a storm). Dam failures have historically led to many human disasters, and so are designed to withstand the worst conceivable storm event, or the **probable maximum flood**. Simply building the dam higher to accommodate the worst possible scenario would be too costly. Thus, every dam has at least one **spillway** that can safely drain water in the event of overwhelming excess.
![[Pasted image 20260705154051.png]]
Most dams have two spillways: a **principal** or **service** spillway that releases normal inflows of water when the reservoir is full, and an **auxiliary** or **emergency** spillway that only sees use during extreme events.
### Spillway Functions
There are three basic functions served by a spillway.
#### Managing the Water Level
The main purpose of a spillway is to prevent the reservoir's water level from breaching a certain threshold. When the water is below the height of the spillway, no water is drained; once the water level is high enough, it begins to pour through the spillway, until the water level falls back down.
![[Pasted image 20260705154556.png]]
Most uncontrolled spillways are **weirs**, which simply allows water to spill over its crest.

![[Pasted image 20260705154707.png|559]]
The **Morning Glory Spillway** in California, and those like it, act like a circular weir at first, but as the bellmouth is overflowed with water, its behavior changes. This type of spillway is typically used in narrow canyons where there isn't room available for a more conventional overflow. Uncontrolled spillways need to be large enough to handle the large water surges that are expected. 

**Controlled spillways**, which require a complex gate mechanism, can remain relatively small and thus reduce costs.
#### Safely Convey Flow Downstream
Spillways need to convey water from the top of the reservoir to a natural water course downstream of the dam. The difference in elevation can be great, allowing water to pick up a lot of force as it accelerates, giving it destructive potential. Thus, a medium in which it is contained is required. In many spillways, this takes the form of a pipe or conduit.

![[Pasted image 20260705155302.png]]
Open channel spillways use a **chute**, which contains the flow between high walls. Conduits and chutes are often made of concrete, able to resist the force of the rapid-flowing water.
#### Energy Dissipation
The speed of flow of water can cause erosion, which can eventually lead to failure of the dam. So, spillways require a way to dissipate the energy before releasing the flow into a natural water course.
![[Pasted image 20260705155600.png]]
Large spillways make use of a **distilling basin** that forces a hydraulic jump. Smaller spillways can make use of simpler methods, like using rock riprap to absorb to help break up the flow, or letting the flow plunge into a deep flow. Once most of the energy has been lost, the water can then calmly and safely flow downstream.
