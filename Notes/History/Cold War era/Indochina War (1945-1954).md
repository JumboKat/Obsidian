### French Indochina
The territory of **French Indochina**, which includes the modern-day territory of Laos, Cambodia, and Vietnam was formed in the late-nineteenth century at the height of European imperialism. Seeking to deny Great Britain—who already controlled Burma (Myanmar), Brunei, and today's Malaysia—additional imperial holdings in South-East Asia, France spreads its influence over independent kingdoms in the region of Indochina, doing so through various means, including via economic concessions, missionaries, aggressive expansion, and imposing protectorates. In 1887, these territories are incorporated into a single colony. Under the guise of 'civilising' its peoples, France exploits the colony for its resources and labour, while supressing local identities.

### Vietnamese Nationalism
French administrators open French schools in their colonies, which teach European science and ethics. While many young Vietnamese subscribe to a superior European government system, some adopt these ideas as the basis of Vietnamese nationalism. One such person is Nguyễn Sinh Cung, or Hồ Chí Minh.

Originating from the countryside, he received a French education in the old imperial capital of Hue and indulges in the ideas of French Enlightenment, though he is critical of French colonial officials and local administrators. He spends many years abroad, including in the U.S. and in Britain, and in 1919, is the head of a Vietnamese delegation to the **Paris Peace Conference**. He is inspired by Woodrow Wilson's ideas of self-determination, and calls for reforms in Vietnam, though not independence at this point. His words fall on deaf ears, as Wilson's ideas do not apply to non-European colonies. This apparent hypocrisy drives him towards Communism, specifically Leninism. 

He helps form the French Communist Party, though discovers that his cohorts care little for colonial matters. Instead, he finds more in common with Soviet and Chinese communist parties, though their ideas are not always in agreement. He champions the idea of conducting a revolution with the Vietnamese peasantry to the disagreement of many Soviet communists, who believe in a revolution led by the urban proletariat. When he forms the Indochinese Communist Party in 1930, its growth is limited by this ongoing clash of ideas. Still, many join the cause, including Võ Nguyên Giáp, a self-taught military strategist who was formerly a teacher.
### Japanese Occupation of Indochina
 In September of 1940, Japan invades North Vietnam to prevent allied supplies from reaching the Chinese via the **Yunnan-Haiphong railway**. It also forces Vichy France to allow it passage through the territory, allowing them to maintain control of the colony in exchange; however in 1941 Japan takes over the rest of Vietnam.
![[Pasted image 20260513090220.png]]

It is at this point that Hồ Chí Minh returns to his home country. He shifts his focus from a Marxist-Leninist ideology to a more general independence movement. In 1941, the Indochina Communist party is absorbed into the larger League for the Independence of Vietnam, or **Viet Minh**, with a goal of uniting all of Vietnam to resist the Japanese and the French. 

The Viet Minh wage a guerrilla-style war against the Japanese, but progress is limited. [[President Roosevelt]], a staunch anti-imperialist, pledges support to the Viet Minh, but it isn't until July of 1945 when a specialist team is sent to train and arm the Viet Minh, a program cut short by Japan's surrender the next month. 

In the meantime, the Viet Minh take control of the Tonkin province, including Hanoi. Having alleviated the 1944/45 famines by distributing food, the Viet Minh are especially popular in the North.
### Reassertion of French Colonialism
While Roosevelt opposed the return of French colonial power to Vietnam, his successor [[Truman]] wants a strong France as an ally against the Soviet Union. Hồ Chí Minh sends letters to Truman asking for recognition, but they are blocked. Instead, the US asserts its neutrality on the issue of restoring French Indochina. 

![[Pasted image 20260513092222.png]]
After [[World War II]], Vietnam is partitioned into the Tonkin (North) and Annam (South) provinces along the 16th parallel, with Nationalist China occupying the North and Great Britain occupying the South. Ho's provisional government tries to spread its influence, but is limited to Tonkin by the Chinese occupation, which includes widespread looting.

At the same time, a Southern Viet Minh Committee is set up in the South's political and economic centre of Saigon. They rush to establish themselves before the British, who are delayed by General Douglas MacArthur, and it is only until he officially accepts the Japanese surrender that they arrive. Eventually, the British, mainly Indian colonial troops, arrive alongside the French. While there is some disagreement among the British, they have decided to support the French, as they fear anti-colonial sentiment spreading to their own colonies that were previously under Japanese occupation. They also want to maintain French Indochina as a trade partner, and to solidify France as a powerful ally. 
### Operation Masterdom
British Major General **Douglas Gracey** is sent to oversee the transition, codenamed **Operation Masterdom**. His orders are relatively simple, and include handling the Japanese surrender, repatriating French prisoners, and disregarding Ho's provisional government. He distrusts the Viet Minh and dismisses them as communists. 

At the time of his arrival, Southern Vietnam is wracked by anarchy, with rioting and looting widespread, and political violence carried out by the Viet Minh against French and Japanese collaborators, despite Hồ Chí Minh's calls for non-violence. Gracey, while biased toward the French cause, urges them to soften their rule. 

Gracey doesn't have the resources that the situation calls for, having only 13,000 troops to process 70,000 Japanese soldiers. With heightened security concerns, he arms French prisoners and former Japanese soldiers to form a makeshift security force. On September 21st, 1945, he enacts new restrictive policies on the Vietnamese, including a curfew, a ban on public demonstrations, and the unauthorized possession of weapons. He is called out by the Viet Minh for having a pro-French bias. Beginning in September, fighting breaks out in Saigon between the Viet Minh and French prisoners backed by Gracey and his British-Indian soldiers. When the fighting starts to get out of hand, he looks towards the Japanese for allegiance, though not all are on his side; many who refused to return to Japan support the Viet Minh via training and directing operations. 
#### Anti-Viet Minh Operations
A brief ceasefire from October 1-10 is broken after a Viet Minh ambush on a British-Indian patrol. Now, professional French troops under General **Philippe Leclerc** make their entrance. 

![[Pasted image 20260620225528.png]]
After mostly securing Saigon, the French, British, and Japanese now seek to take "The Triangle," a zone in where Japanese prisoners can be concentrated and processed, but is currently held by the Viet Minh. From October 23rd to 25th, the British clear the three towns that form the triangle and begin processing Japanese prisoners. On October 24th, the French capture towns to the south of Saigon, while Saigon's commercial district is torched by British troops. On October 29th, the newly-formed armoured car column **GATEFORCE**, which includes some Japanese infantrymen, make a pre-emptive move east against the Viet Minh. At *Xuân Lộc*, they clash with the Viet Minh at a rubber plantation, dissolving the Viet Minh in the are.
#### British Exit
The British now hand over security to the French. On November 30th, Gracey formally accepts the Japanese surrender, and by late December, the British begin their exit of Indochina; they had underestimated the local resistance, and were facing mounting political backlash at home. Gracey had also complained to LeClerc about French racism toward British-Indian troops, who themselves were questioning their support of Britain against a fellow colonized people.

While it is true that British were much more effective in combatting the Viet Minh than their French and American successors down the line, it should be noted that the Viet Minh were not as organized, well-trained, and well-armed as they would be later on in the 1950s, and even later as the Viet Cong. There was also more division among the Vietnamese between Northerners and Southerners. There was also much less control of operations and a lack of an overall military strategy, as leaders like Hồ Chí Minh and Võ Nguyên Giáp were all the way in the North. Additionally, Viet Minh still stuck to conventional warfare at this time, whereas in later conflicts they would rely heavily on guerilla tactics. On the other hand, British Indian and Gurkha troops were well-experienced from serving in Burma and other occupation duties. British commanders exercised caution, always ensuring a line of retreat, and received much support from the Japanese. Above all, the British never sought to entirely pacify the Vietnamese people; their only concern was to process Japanese prisoners and restore French power, and were content once these objectives were realized. The idea of war was never on the table, as even the Vietnamese in the south were still hesitant, and many, including Hồ Chí Minh, still held out hope for a diplomatic solution with the French.
### French Takeover
With the [[Chinese Civil War]] intensifying, the Chinese Nationalists in the North change their policy and agree to allow French return on the condition that they open diplomacy with Hồ. However, in March 1946, the French demand control of the North anyway, and occupy Hanoi. While they make a deal with Hồ Chí Minh, they recognize his Vietnamese Republic, but only as a free state within the new French Union, a deal the Viet Minh are not satisfied with. The French, while asserting control, are slow to implement the promised reforms and referendum. 

French High Commissioner Admiral **Georges Thierry D'Argenlieu** opposes cooperation with Ho and solely believes in using military force to control the Viet Minh, while General Philippe Leclerc wants a more diplomatic solution, and makes pleas to Paris to agree with his point of view. Post-war France, however, is more focused on economic recovery and political stability in Europe. D'Argenlieu is keen on preserving France's global power by tightening its control over Indochina. 

![[Pasted image 20260705162255.png]]D'Argenlieu raises tensions when he establishes the puppet state of Chochinchina in Southern Vietnam under Bao Dai, the former emperor who led the previous Japanese puppet regime. D'Argenlieu states that this new province lies outside the scope of the agreement with Ho.

With the Vietnamese dissatisfied with the French deal, violence breaks out. In November 1946, the French bombard the port at Haiphong in response to militant resistance, killing 6,000 civilians. Both sides scramble for diplomacy, with French diplomat **Jean Sainteny** seeking a long-term solution with Hồ Chí Minh, while his detractors paint Ho as a radical communist secretly preparing for war. The final set of negotiations fail in December 1946.
### Outbreak of War
On December 19, 1946, Vietnamese militants attack the French in Hanoi. Many suspect that the Viet Minh were not involved in these attacks, with Hồ Chí Minh himself taken by surprise. The response from the French was heavy-handed; By 1947, French armour, aircraft, paratroopers, colonial troops, and the **French Foreign Legion** are arriving in increasing numbers. Both sides mainly use World War II-era weapons, including captured weapons. Initially, the French make use of captured Japanese and recaptured French tanks. Later on, US equipment passed down by French armies in Europe begin to arrive, including M4 Shermans, M24 Chaffee tanks, M3 halftracks, and amphibious vehicles. With their enemy's newfound strength, the Viet Minh are largely restricted to guerilla warfare. For the next two years, they conduct a war of insurgency across Indochina, especially in the north near the border with China.

Although D'Argenlieu had been recalled from Indochina by this point, the French seek to continue his plans of a separate Vietnamese state under Bao Dai, naming him the leader of the State of Vietnam based in Saigon in 1949; a French attempt at creating an alternative to Ho's Vietnamese Republic in the north. He earns little support from the Vietnamese, however, having served as a puppet for the Japanese and French and generally lacking confidence.

Having pushed out the Viet Minh from major cities and urban areas, the French are quick to call a victory. However, the US refuses to recognize Bao Dai, and the Viet Minh control around 55% of villages, with French logistics already stretched to their limits.
### Battle of Route Coloniale 4
In 1949, the Chinese Civil War concludes in a communist victory, and the Chinese Communist Party are eager to support Hồ Chí Minh, seeing as they have much in common, and that it would help shore up China's southern border. By 1950, Chinese advisors and weapons are flowing into Tonkin to support the Viet Minh's forces. 

![[Pasted image 20260705164209.png]]French Chief of the General Staff **George Revers** wants to abandon frontier positions and consolidate them in the more defensible *Red River Delta*, but he is overruled. Instead, receives orders from Paris to hold the *Cao Bang-Lang Son Ridge*, a series of fortifications that sit along the vital French logistical route *Colonial Route No. 4*. While these had previously withstood attacks by the Viet Minh, in September 1949 the Viet Minh launched an assault of 16,000 troops with much more sophistication and organization. *Dong Khe* falls after two days of fighting, and soon after a French relief force is sent from the Red River Delta. On October 3rd, French troops retreat from the safety of their forts to meet up with the relief unit. Along the way, they are ambushed by Viet Minh and the two French columns never meet. By October 17th, all of the fortifications along the route have fallen.
### De Lattre's Campaign
With French control of the Red River Delta under threat, the French send a new High Commissioner, General **Jean de Lattre de Tassigny**, who fought with the Free French during WWII and beside the U.S. during their invasion of France and Germany. Under his leadership, the French strengthen their position and fiercely defend the so-called *de Lattre Line*. Viet Minh commander Giap seeks to go on the offensive after their recent victories. Chinese advisors recommend them to draw on their manpower superiority and conduct human wave attacks along the line, a tactic that saw great success against the poorly-equipped Nationalist Chinese, but was dispersed with ease by French firepower. Throughout 1951, the Viet Minh suffered heavy casualties as their assaults were broken up by French artillery and airstrikes, including with napalm. Even worse for the Viet Minh, the outbreak of the Korean war draws attention of the Chinese elsewhere.

The French, having established a stable position, now seek to expand their operations. Because the war hadn't been officially declared, de Lattre receives little support from Paris, and any troops from Europe consists entirely of volunteers (though not only from France, but Germany, Hungary, Spain, and Yugoslavia). These make up half of the overall French force, with the remainder being colonial troops and local Vietnamese, especially those of ethic minorities. These new troops allow de Lattre to free up the French legionnaires and paratroopers for sweeping offensive operations across Tonkin, targeting training bases, depots, and propaganda centres. Having lost most of his troops in hard-on assaults, and with Chinese support wavering, Giap's strategy makes a return to guerilla tactics. Both sides at this point expect the war to be long and drawn out.
### U.S. Support
With France occupied in conflicts elsewhere, de Lattre looks for foreign support in Indochina, in particular from the United States. To entice their involvement, he rebrands the conflict as a defensive struggle against communism, during a time where fears of communism are growing in the United States. In 1950, the U.S. recognizes Bao Dai's puppet government, but with their attention focused on Korea, they only provide a $10 million aid packet and a few military advisors, and when de Lattre passes away due to cancer in 1951, the idea of more U.S. aid is crippled even further. Many, like future president John F. Kennedy, are hesitant to support what they see as a war of colonial oppression.
### Operation Lorraine
![[Pasted image 20260707154534.png]]
Giap continues his strategy of avoiding confrontation with the French and goading them to overextend. In late October 1952, the French launch **Operation Lorraine**, their largest sweep into Viet Minh territory. 300,000 troops capture supply depots as they make their way north nearly 160 km away from their defenses in the Delta. Meeting up with paratroopers, they capture *Phu Doan* on November 9th against light resistance, after which they spread out to other targets, leaving them in a vulnerable position. On November 14th, the Viet Minh ambush the French in a narrow gorge near *Chian Muong*. The French are forced to retreat back to the de Lattre line as they are harried by the Viet Minh, suffering losses along the way.
### Battle of Na San
![[Pasted image 20260707155318.png]]
With newfound confidence after their recent victory, the Viet Minh now look to *Na San*, where a French have established a garrison, fire base, and airstrip. It is well-defended, and capable of receiving air supplies from Hanoi.

![[Pasted image 20260707155418.png]]
Na San's commander, General **Jean Gilles**, has designed the defenses according to **hérisson** (hedgehog) tactics, with its centre surrounded by 30 armed points forming an inner and outer ring. Many points is protected by barbed wire, with many positioned to create interlocking fields. Na San was deliberately designed to goad the Viet Minh into launching costly attacks. From November to December, Giap attempts several assaults on the compound, but suffer heavy casualties at the hands of French artillery and airstrikes.

Giap once again recognizes the ineffectiveness of the Viet Minh in conventional assaults. The French have found a winning formula using airstrikes and artillery; if they can establish positions supplied by air (called 'air-heads') and supported with heavy firepower, they can break any Viet Minh attack whilst suffering minimal losses, and follow up with a French offensive afterward to finish off the Viet Minh. French planners decide that the location of their next air-head will be the small, isolated village of *Dien Bien Phu*.
### Stalemate
By 1953, the conflict had come to a stalemate, with both sides having scored significant victories against the other, but had yet to find the killing blow. In May of 1953, General **Henri Navarre** is sent to find an honourable way to end the war, but he needs significant victories in order to secure diplomatic leverage over the Viet Minh. He decides to pull back his forces from various garrisons and consolidate them for one big offensive, training local Vietnamese to take over garrison duties. He also requests more aid from the U.S. At this time, former U.S. WWII general **Dwight D. Eisenhower** was president, and he was concerned with the spread of communism in both Europe and Asia. The Korean war wraps up in July of 1953 ending in a stalemate. It showed that while international intervention could be effective in stemming the tide of communism, it came at a huge cost and no definitive victory. It also flipped American opinions on Hồ Chí Minh; while previous administrations were sympathetic to the Vietnamese, Eisenhower's administration sees his movement as yet another branch of Soviet communism. The U.S. agrees to send a $400 million aid package on the condition that the French cease all negotiation with Hồ Chí Minh and accepts West German remilitarization in Europe.
### Battle of Dien Bien Phu
![[Pasted image 20260707161727.png]]
The Viet Minh now begin extending their operations into Laos, which would extend the war out of the range of the French forces consolidated in the Red River Delta. With his hand forced, Navarre decides to seize the strategically important village of Dien Bien Phu in an airborne attack in order to threaten the supply route to Laos. On November 20th, 1953, six French parachute battalions are dropped into the area, encountering light resistance, but still more than they had anticipated. After securing the area, the French airdrop bulldozers and other equipment to create an air-head, with the goal of making Dien Bien Phu a forward base from which raids and patrols will be made into the Viet Minh rear areas.
#### French Base at Dien Bien Phu
![[Pasted image 20260707161927.png]]
In early 1954, they turn the village into a fortified compound, with nine defensive bastions; most are flat on the ground next to the airstrips, with others positioned on the hills above. They then deliver troops, equipment, artillery, tanks, and bombers, with the tanks being disassembled for delivery and reassembled on the premises. They even fly in Vietnamese and Algerian women for the base brothel. By January, around 10,800 troops had been flown in.

The Viet Minh quickly recognize the base location's many flaws, however. Dien Bien Phu sits 275 km away from Hanoi, making air supply much slower to arrive. It is also out of the range of the main French ground force in the Red River Delta, and because it was designed as primarily an offensive encampment, its defenses are lacking. There are large gaps between bastions, many of which are far away and isolated. The artillery is poorly protected, and there are too few guns given the size of the camp. The greatest weakness of all, however, was the fact that much of it, including the air strip, was overlooked by forested hills, allowing the Viet Minh an ideal position firing and spotting French movements, whilst hiding their own. While the French are not ignorant to these issues, they downplay their impact. Many local aircraft, including early helicopters, can provide constant air support, and they consider the hills to be impassable for heavy equipment. The Viet Minh had also not yet shown a capacity for dealing with French artillery and air support, and isolated bastions would merely lure them in for destruction.

Giap moves 50,000 troops—about 1/3rd of his total army—into the area, and with the Korean War now over, he receives much more support from the Chinese. Advisors train the Viet Minh on using heavy weapons, artillery, and in engineering, and provide them with high-calibre artillery, including captured American models, as well as sophisticated anti-aircraft weapons.
