**LEDs** (*light emitting diodes*) work in the same way as diodes; they both are comprised of a semiconductor sandwiched between electrical connectors. They both emit photons, but an LED emits photons within the visible range of 400-700 nm. Within the semiconductor, electrons combine with holes and emit a photon in the process. In electrical diagrams, LEDs are represented by a diode symbol with two upward arrows representing the emittance of light.
### Visible Light
![[Pasted image 20260827162052.png]]
Colours are distinguished by their wavelength. The wavelength of an FM radio signal photon is much larger at around 3 metres. A wifi signal is around 6 cm, while x-rays come at 0.01 nm. These are all invisible to us as they sit outside the visible spectrum.

![[Pasted image 20260827162328.png]]
The LED used by TV remotes emit infrared light at 940 nm. It is invisible to the human eye, however your phone camera can capture it.
### Heat Efficiency
![[Pasted image 20260827162954.png]]
Standard diodes use a different semiconductor material that produces photons near infrared range that are absorbed by the shell casing and causes a build up of heat. An incandescent light bulb relies on the collision of electrons and atoms to create heat, heating up the filament and causing it to glow. 

An LED however does not rely on heat to produce light and are thus more efficient than incandescent lightbulbs. And unlike standard diodes, they produce cold light (non-infrared).
### LED Types
#### Through-hole
![[Pasted image 20260827163324.png]]
The most common and widely available LED is the 5mm through-hole type, though 3mm and 10mm versions also exist. They can be bought in bulk very cheaply and can be inserted into test boards or soldered into PCBs.

They are typically dome-shaped but can also be square.

#### SMDs
SMDs (*surface mounted device*) are significantly smaller than through-hole LEDs and are designed to be soldered onto boards for compact designs. 

![[Pasted image 20260827163659.png]]
They are used for LED lightbulbs, specifically blue LEDs; a layer of yellow phosphorous is placed over it, resulting in a white light being produced.
#### High-power
![[Pasted image 20260827163927.png]]
Many LEDs together are used to make high-powered LEDs like torches and floodlights. They can produce extremely bright lights that can be seen from very far away.
### Producing Different Colours
![[Pasted image 20260827164107.png]]While LEDs come in cases of different colours, these are only used to inform us of what colour light will be emitted; the colour is actually determined by the material used in the semiconductor layer.
### Distinguishing the Anode and Cathode
![[Pasted image 20260827164219.png]]
An LED will only illuminate when the **anode** is connected to the positive and the **cathode** to the negative. 

![[Pasted image 20260827164421.png|360]]![[Pasted image 20260827164502.png|333]]
The anode can usually distinguished as the longer terminal, however if the connectors have been trimmed, the case has a flat edge located on the cathode side. Additionally, within the case are two plates; the larger plate denotes the cathode.

![[Pasted image 20260827164725.png|351]]![[Pasted image 20260827164703.png|304]]
In SMDs, a marking at the top right can indicate either the anode or cathode; you must either consult the manufacturer's datasheet or test it yourself. The same applies for the marking on the underside.
