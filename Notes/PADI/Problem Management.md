Though diving actually has a better safety record than many other sports and activities, there will still be hazards and risks you will need to contend with. Following guidelines and procedures can help reduce risk, but you can never fully eliminate it.
### Surface Problems
Most diver-in-distress situations occur at the surface. If an issue at the surface occurs, establish buoyancy by inflating your BCD or dropping your weights; try to conserve energy. Common surface issues include overexertion, leg muscle cramps, and choking on water.
- Stay relaxed to avoid overexertion. If you begin to overexert, ensure buoyancy, stop and rest. 
- Leg cramps result from involuntary muscle contractions, and tend to be more frequent if you have not been diving for a while as your muscles aren't used to swimming with fins.
- Airway control helps to prevent choking on inhaled water, along with keeping your mask on and your snorkel/regulator in your mouth. If you do choke on water, be sure you are buoyant, and cough into your regulator or snorkel while holding it.
#### Assisting a Responsive Diver
A **responsive** diver is one who is breathing, alert, and active. They can be in control (not panicked), or out of control (panicked). Those who are in control will usually signal or ask for assistance. They will appear more relaxed and are deliberate in their actions. They typically respond to and follow instructions, while keeping their gear in place and establishing buoyancy.

Panicked divers have been overcome with fear and resort to instinctive, ineffective, and often repetitive actions. Fearing drowning, they will struggle violently and exhaust their energy trying to stay at the surface. They typically fail to establish buoyancy, and may try to climb onto anything or anyone nearby. Their masks may be on their foreheads or gone altogether, and don't use their breathing equipment. They usually have wide, unseeing eyes, quick and jerky movements, and are unresponsive to instructions. They respond to fear and pay little attention to anything else; they are in immediate help as they can exhaust themselves, at which point they are at risk of sinking and drowning. 

There are four basic steps for assisting a responsive diver, whether in control or panicked, at the surface:
1. Establish buoyancy for both you and the diver, ensuring neither of you will sink. Ideally, you will throw or extend a floatation device to the diver. If you must make contact, ensure you are buoyant first, then inflate the diver's BCD and/or drop their weights.
2. Calm them by reassuring them and offering encouragement. 
3. Help reestablish breathing control; have them take slow, deep breaths to restore self-control. If possible, give them a chance to rest and recover.
4. Assist them to the boat or shore as necessary. Even if unpanicked, they may need help due to leg cramps or exhaustion.
#### Assisting an Unresponsive Diver
An **unresponsive** (unconscious) diver requires immediate help. They may be unresponsive due to inhaling water, extreme fatigue, heart attack or lung overexpansion injuries, etc. Panic, inefficient breathing, throat blockage, and exhausting can also be contributing factors.

Your first concern is to check for breathing, and if they are not, to begin rescue breathing. Do this after ensuring both of you have positive buoyancy. Assume that a diver who is floating in an odd position and is not moving needs help until you confirm otherwise. 

There are four basic steps to assist an unresponsive diver at the surface:
1. Establish buoyancy for you and the victim.
2. Call for help to the boat or shore; others can assist by helping to tow the victim, preparing emergency equipment, alerting EMS, etc.
3. Check for breathing, and begin providing rescue breaths if necessary as you tow them to safety.
4. Continue rescue breathing if needed during tow, and help them get out of the water. Continue rescue procedures once out of the water.
### Underwater Problem Management
Many underwater problems can be prevented or controlled by following three basic guidelines:
1. Stay relaxed while you dive.
2. Plan out your air use and monitor it closely.
3. Dive within the limits of your experience and training.
#### Free-flowing Regulator
Modern regulators are designed to be highly reliable, and even in the event of failure, they release air continuously (called a **freeflow**), preventing the cutoff of oxygen. When breathing from a free-flowing regulator, do not seal your mouth to the mouthpiece, as there is some risk of lung overexpansion injury if the flow rate is high, but also because the flowing air makes it difficult to keep the regulator in your mouth. It can also flood your mask. 

To breathe, hold the second stage in your hand and press the mouthpiece outside your lips, letting the excess air escape freely, and 'sip' from it. You should begin your ascent as soon as possible, as freeflow quickly drains your air supply.
#### Running Low On/Out of Air Underwater
If you run out of air before reaching your planned ascent point, ascend where you air while you still have sufficient air. There are four options to consider when making the ascent on low air:
1. Make a normal ascent: do this if your cylinder is low on air but not completely empty. As you ascend, more air will be accessible as the surrounding water pressure decreases. Breathe lightly and continuously, making a controlled and continuous ascent to the surface; do not attempt a safety stop.
2. Ascend using an alternate air source: this is the best option if an alternate air source provided by a buddy is available.
3. Make a **Controlled Emergency Swimming Ascent (CESA)**: The best choice if you are completely out of air, no deeper than approx. 6-9m/20-30ft, and the surface is closer than your buddy(ies) or another diver, and you have no alternate air source. Simply look up and swim to the surface while making a continuous "ahhhhh" sound into your regulator; this ensures that you exhale expanding gas and avoid lung overexpansion injury. Keep the regulator in your mouth and do not drop weights to start the ascent. Maintain a safe rate of ascension.
4. Make a buoyant emergency ascent: Do this as a last resort; your buddies are too far, you have no alternate air source, and you are so deep that you cannot reach the surface any other way. Perform a CESA, except this time dropping all of your weights. This is the most risky option as you will likely exceed the safe rate of ascent. As you near the surface, you can flare out your limbs to create drag and slow your ascent.
If the need arises, you can use a combination of these options. Once at the surface, establish buoyancy, but you will have to drop your weights or inflate your BCD orally (you cannot use the low-pressure inflator (LPI) since your cylinder is empty)

Other issues like entanglement and overexertion can occur, and techniques to handle them have already been covered elsewhere.
#### Unresponsive Diver Underwater
The priority when helping an unresponsive diver underwater is to get them to the surface. Follow these procedures:
1. Swim the diver to the surface, using their BCD and/or dropping their weights as necessary.
2. If the diver's regulator is in their mouth, hold it there. If not, do not waste time trying to replace it.
3. Ascend at a safe rate. If the diver's buoyancy is too great for a safe ascent, let the victim go. Finish your own safe ascent and continue the rescue at the surface.
4. Once at the surface, follow the priorities and procedures for an unresponsive diver at the surface.
### First Responder Care for Diving-Related Emergencies
A diver who was unresponsive is a serious medical emergency. Becoming unresponsive underwater (including drowning), pressure-related injuries and other unrelated medical conditions (e.g. heart attack) can result in these signs (what you observe) and symptoms (what the victim feels):
- difficulty breathing
- unconsciousness
- unclear thinking
- visual problems
- paralysis
- chest pain
- lowered alertness
- cardiac and respiratory arrest
#### Assisting an Unresponsive Diver Out of the Water
After removing the diver out of the water, your priority is to ensure they are breathing and to contact EMS. Follow these general steps:
1. Keep the airway open and check for breathing.
2. Provide rescue breathing or CPR as necessary. Do not preform abdominal thrusts unless you are unable to provide rescue breathing due to a suspected obstruction. Inhaled water does not prevent rescue breaths, and you don't have to try to clear it.
3.  If they are unresponsive but breathing, lay them level on the left side (recovery position). This position is superseded by the need to transport the diver to safety or providing CPR.
4. Monitor their breathing frequently.
5. If they have regained consciousness, keep them lying down comfortably.
6. Administer emergency oxygen ASAP.
7. Keep them still and maintain a normal body temperature.
8. Continue to provide care until EMS arrives.
9. If you cannot accompany them to medical care, write down as much contextual info as possible about the diver and the dive, and attach it to them in a conspicuous place.

Even if they have regained consciousness, any diver who was at one point unresponsive requires a medical examination. Some conditions, such as drowning, can have delayed consequences.
