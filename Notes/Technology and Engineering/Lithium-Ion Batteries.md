All batteries have a negative terminal (**anode**) and a positive terminal (**cathode**). Negatively-charged electrons flow from the anode to the cathode. In a **lithium-ion battery**, electrons come from the element lithium. 
### The Anode
At the anode, lithium is stored between layers of carbon graphite. Graphite has a crystal structure of layered planes that allows lithium to be intercalated, or wedged in between the layers. The graphite provides a stable space for storing the lithium. As lithium's atomic number is 3, it has one unstable valence electron. When there is an available path from the anode to the cathode, the electron separates from the lithium and travels to the other terminal. At the same time, the lithium becomes a +1 positively-charged ion and leaves the graphite lattice. When a lot of lithium ions leave at the same time, a flow of electrons is produced.
### The Cathode
In batteries used in personal consumer electronics, like smartphones, tablets, and cameras, limited space is available. Lithium Cobalt Oxide is used for its high specific energy, meaning it can store a lot of power while remaining small and lightweight. The tradeoff is that it has a relatively shorter lifespan, lower thermal stability, and is more expensive when compared to other cathode materials.

In the cathode is a lattice of cobalt and oxygen. The cobalt, having lost its electrons to the oxygen, are positively +4 charged, and are very willing to accept electrons. Since lithium wants to lose electrons, electrons from the anode travel to the cathode. As more and more electrons flow in one direction, the cathode begins to become more negatively-charged, while the anode becomes more and more positively-charged.
